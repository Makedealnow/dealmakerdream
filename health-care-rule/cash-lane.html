Health Care Rule Kit

The Cash-Pay Lane

Cash lane — paying for your own care

Watch Katie first — she walks you through this whole lane.

The lane where you’re not almost a customer. You are one.

And hear this first: this lane is a watch, not a job. If everything goes right, there is almost nothing to do — the process runs on its own. Your only work is knowing what “smooth” looks like, so you notice the moment it doesn’t. The plays for that moment are written out below.

First, three quick questions

1. No insurance at all right now? Then you’re a cash-pay patient in full. Everything in this lane applies to you as written — and the written estimate in Step 1 below is your single most important right.

2. You have a plan, but you’re paying cash for this care? Then you’re in this lane for this service. Two cautions: cash you pay generally does not count toward your plan’s deductible or out-of-pocket maximum — run the math before you decide, not after — and tell the provider your choice clearly, before the service, and get their answer in your notes. Some providers must bill your insurance if they know you have it.

3. Between plans? Your self-pay rights cover the care you receive while uninsured — the estimate right does not depend on why you have no plan. If a bill from that window arrives later, it’s judged by this lane’s rules: the estimate you were given and the prices that were published. Keep this lane’s paper trail exactly as written.

Covered by Medicaid or a public program instead? Wrong lane — yours is stronger in several places. Go to the welfare lane.

Your facts stay yours: you place yourself, here, by reading. Nothing is uploaded, submitted, or sent anywhere. There is no account and no form — your answers exist in exactly one place: your hands.

No insurer in the middle. No network, no authorization, no claim. There is a seller, a price, and a buyer: you. That means you’re owed prices and estimates before the service — and this short lane shows you how to get them, in writing.

Katie’s rule for this lane

THE RULE: she has the right to a good-faith estimate before scheduled care. If she is uninsured or paying for a service herself, federal law — the No Surprises Act — requires the provider to give her that estimate, in writing.

THE CHECK: the estimate in writing, kept. Dated, with the name of whoever sent it, filed in her folder before the service happens — and a matching estimate from every other provider who will bill her separately.

THE MEANING: a final bill $400 or more over the estimate is federally disputable. That is not a complaint and not a favor she asks for — it is a process the law built for exactly that gap, and her kept estimate is what opens it.

The steps

Step 1 — The good-faith estimate: your price, in writing, before

This right belongs to self-pay patients specifically. Under the federal No Surprises Act, providers must give you a good-faith estimate of the expected charges for scheduled care — in writing — when you schedule it far enough ahead (generally, services scheduled at least a few business days out) or when you ask for one. It should list the items and services expected, with their prices, including the providers expected to be involved. And if a final bill from a provider on that estimate comes in $400 or more above it, federal law gives you a dispute process built for exactly that. But it runs on the paper. No estimate, no dispute.

Treat the estimate as your insurance card. In the insured lane, the card is what the whole system keys off. In this lane, the written estimate does that job: it is the document that names your care, your providers, and your price before anyone starts — the one page every later conversation points back to. Photograph it the day it arrives and keep the photo on your phone, the way the insured lane keeps its plan card. If a number on paper and a number someone says out loud ever disagree, the paper wins.

DO THIS: For every scheduled service — procedure, scan, surgery, therapy series — request the estimate in writing, before the service — from the facility, and from every other provider who will bill you separately (surgeon, anesthesiologist, lab, imaging). The main estimate is not the whole bill; the side bills are where estimates quietly stop covering you.

SAY THIS: “I’m self-pay. Before I schedule, I need my good-faith estimate in writing — the full expected charge for this service, and a list of any other providers who will bill me separately, so I can get their estimates too. Please send it to me before the appointment, not at check-in.”

KEEP THIS PAPER: The estimate itself, dated, with the name of whoever sent it. When the bill arrives, this page is the first thing it gets compared against — Step 5 and the worksheet exist for exactly that comparison.

Step 2 — Price shopping: published prices, and the discount nobody volunteers

Hospitals are required by federal price-transparency rules to publish their prices — including the discounted cash price — and to display several hundred “shoppable” services you can schedule in advance. The files are enormous and were plainly not designed for a person at a kitchen table. You don’t read them. You use them, one service at a time.

DO THIS: Before scheduled hospital care, find the hospital’s price transparency page (search the hospital’s name plus “price transparency” or “standard charges”). Look up your specific service and find the discounted cash price — not the gross “chargemaster” charge, which is the sticker nobody is supposed to pay. If the hospital offers a price-estimator tool for self-pay patients, run your service through it too, and note what it says.

DO THIS — compare the building, not just the price: for the same scheduled service — a scan, a lab panel, a procedure — price it at the hospital and at an independent facility that does only that service (an independent imaging center, a standalone lab, an outpatient surgery center). The identical service can differ by hundreds of dollars between facilities in the same town, because a hospital’s price carries the whole building with it. In this lane, nobody has to ask a network’s permission — comparison shopping actually works here.

DO THIS — always ask for the prompt-pay discount: most providers have a self-pay rate — a prompt-pay or cash discount off the sticker price — and almost none of them lead with it. The first number you’re quoted is usually the chargemaster number: the highest number in the building, the one insurers negotiate down from. A customer asks. Ask before the service, while you still have the only leverage a customer ever has: the ability to schedule somewhere else.

SAY THIS: “I’m paying for this myself, without insurance. What is your cash price — your self-pay or prompt-pay rate — for [service]? Is that the full price, including the facility and any other providers who’ll bill me? And can I get that price in writing before I schedule?” If you found a published cash price, name it: “Your published cash price for this service is [amount] — I have it dated [date]. Is that the price I’ll be charged?”

DO THIS: Ask at least one other provider the same question for any service big enough to matter.

KEEP THIS PAPER: A screenshot or printout of every published price you found, with the date — prices get updated, and the dated copy is what was true when you relied on it — plus every quote: the price, what it includes, the date, and the name of the person who quoted it. Quotes you can’t point to later are conversations; quotes in your log are the opening position of every later conversation. If a hospital has no findable price file, or the file omits your service, write that down too, with the date — a missing price is a deviation (see this lane’s lists).

Step 3 — The all-in price agreement: the whole price, in writing, before

DO THIS: Never pay the first number. The first number is a starting position — theirs. The estimate (Step 1), the published price (Step 2), and the cash quote (Step 2) are yours. When they disagree, the lowest documented number is the one to talk from.

DO THIS: Get the agreed price in writing before treatment, whenever the care is schedulable. One page or one email. And the writing has to name what the price includes — line by line: the facility, the provider’s own work, anesthesia, lab work, imaging — and who, if anyone, still bills separately on top of it. This page converts a negotiation into an agreement — and an agreement is what you hold them to when the bill says something else.

The partial-price trap: a quote that covers only the facility — or only the doctor — sounds like the price of the service and is not. The rest arrives later, as separate bills, at prices nobody quoted. So never accept a number until the writing answers one question completely: is this everything, and if it isn’t, exactly what else is there, and what will that cost? A price that won’t say what it includes is not yet a price.

SAY THIS: “Before I schedule, I need the agreed price in writing: the service, the total price, what it includes — facility, provider, anesthesia, lab — and the names of anyone who will bill me separately, with their prices. Email is fine.”

DO THIS — deposits: a provider may ask for a deposit or payment before scheduled care. Before you pay anything, establish — in writing — whether the amount is a deposit against the estimate or a settled full price, what happens if the final charges differ, and what the refund process is if the service doesn’t happen. A deposit handed over without those answers is the cash lane’s version of signing the clipboard unread.

SAY THIS (the deposit conversation): “Before I pay a deposit, I need three things in writing: whether this is a deposit or the full agreed price, what happens if the final bill is different from the estimate, and how a refund works if the service is cancelled. Email is fine.”

KEEP THIS PAPER: The written agreed price and every deposit receipt, in your folder.

Step 4 — Scope changes: a new service means a new conversation — before, not after

Sometimes the plan changes once care starts: a test is added, a finding leads to a second procedure, a service you never discussed appears on the schedule. Some of that is medicine, and this kit takes no position on the medicine. The billing rule is separate, and it is simple: a new service is a new price conversation, held before the service — not a surprise discovered on the bill after it.

DO THIS: Whenever a service is added or changed before it happens, ask for the updated picture before you agree: what the new service is, what it costs at your cash rate, whether it changes the estimate, and who bills it. Ask for the updated estimate in writing covering the new scope. If the change is proposed while you’re already at the facility and it isn’t urgent, you can say you want the price first — a schedulable addition can wait for its number.

SAY THIS: “That’s a new service, and it wasn’t in my estimate. Before I agree to it, I need its cash price and an updated good-faith estimate in writing that includes it — and the name of whoever will bill me for it.”

KEEP THIS PAPER: The updated estimate beside the original, each dated. Two estimates for one episode is normal when the scope changed in the open — what is not normal is a changed scope with only the original paper and a bigger bill.

Step 5 — The bill, measured against the estimate

When the bill arrives, it gets measured — against the estimate, the published price, and the written agreement, line by line. Not against your memory, and not against what feels fair.

DO THIS: First, demand the shape that can be checked: if a bill arrives as a single lump number, ask for it itemized, line by line. A lump sum cannot be checked against an estimate; an itemized bill can. Never pay a lump. Then compare, provider by provider: each billed line against the same provider’s estimate, and the totals against each other. The worksheet at the bottom of this page is built for exactly this subtraction.

IF THE DIFFERENCE IS $400 OR MORE: for that provider, the federal patient-provider dispute resolution process opens — run through the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services, built for exactly this gap. The $400 threshold and the process details are federal rules as of this kit’s writing; they can change, and the current version lives on the federal health care website (search “patient-provider dispute resolution”). Federal law gives you 120 calendar days from the date on the initial bill to start the dispute — write that deadline on your log the week the bill arrives, and don’t let it drift. A bill $400 or more over the estimate, per provider, is not a bill to pay and not a bill to ignore: it’s a dispute to open.

IF IT’S JUST WRONG — below $400, or wrong in some other way: say so in writing. Name the line, name what your paper shows, and ask them to correct it or explain it in writing. And while a part of the bill is in written disagreement, pay the undisputed part — the lines that check out — and say in the same writing that the rest is disputed and why. Paying what’s right while disputing what isn’t is the strongest position a customer can stand in: nobody can call you unwilling, and the disagreement is on paper, dated, in both files.

SAY THIS (in writing): “I am disputing the charges identified in this letter, which do not match the good-faith estimate and agreed price I hold. I am paying the undisputed amount of [amount] today. Please correct the bill, or explain each disputed charge in writing, identifying the record that supports it.”

KEEP THIS PAPER: Every bill, itemized; your comparison notes; and a copy of every disagreement letter, with its date. All of it stays in the folder past the final payment — a paid bill can be re-billed or sent to collections in error, and your folder is the whole answer.

The financial assistance door — ask before you pay

For a big hospital bill, this may be the most valuable page in the lane. Nonprofit hospitals are required to have financial assistance (charity care) policies — income-based help with the bill — and eligibility often reaches further up the income scale than people assume. Applying is paperwork, not begging: it is a published policy with criteria, and a self-pay patient facing a large bill is exactly who it is written for.

DO THIS: Always apply before paying a big hospital bill. Ask for the policy and the application before the service if you can, and after the bill if you couldn’t. Ask even if you have income — even if you assume you earn too much. The policy’s criteria decide, not your assumption, and not the billing clerk’s.

SAY THIS: “Before I pay anything on this bill, I want to apply under your financial assistance policy. Please send me the policy and the application, and note my account that an application is coming.”

KEEP THIS PAPER: The policy, your application, and the answer — all in the folder. The shared road develops this fully; in this lane, just never let a big hospital bill pass without the question being asked.

EMERGENCIES — the honest exception: you don’t negotiate in an emergency — you get care. Federal law (EMTALA) requires hospital emergency departments to screen and stabilize you regardless of ability to pay; emergency care can’t be denied for money, and that duty is not conditioned on a deposit conversation. Your customer rights in an emergency are mostly after the fact, and they’re real: the itemized bill, the financial-assistance screening, the estimate rules for any follow-up care that gets scheduled, and the billing protections the shared road walks later. Emergencies skip this lane’s front end; they do not skip its paper trail — start the folder with whatever the emergency produced.

Your folder — in this lane, the record is entirely yours

In the insured lane, three parties keep files: you, the provider, and the plan. In this lane there are only two parties, and the provider’s file is a billing system. Your folder is the only complete record of what was quoted, agreed, and charged — which makes it more important here, not less. Four kinds of paper, in order:

1. Estimates (Step 1) — dated, with who sent them.

2. Published prices (Step 2) — screenshots, dated.

3. Quotes and agreements (Steps 2–3) — the log of who quoted what, and the written agreed price.

4. Bills and receipts — every bill, in its itemized version.

DO THIS: Start the folder now — paper or digital, one place — labeled with the provider and the date of service for each episode of care. Every later step in this kit, including the dispute in Step 5, runs on what’s in it. Keep all of it past the final payment.

If this happens — the deviation plays

The steps above are the smooth path. These cards are not the path — they are what to do the moment the path bends. Each one is a deviation: something the other side was supposed to do, didn’t, and now answers for.

If this happens: the estimate never comes, comes only at check-in, or comes as spoken words

DO THIS: Don’t let check-in be the first price conversation. Repeat Step 1’s words, in writing where you can — email or portal message — and log who you asked, when, and what they said. For schedulable care, you can wait for the paper before you proceed; that waiting is the right working, not a delay you caused.

SAY THIS: “I asked for my good-faith estimate in writing before the appointment. I don’t have it yet. Please send it before we go ahead — and note in my file that I asked, and when.”

If this happens: “We don’t give prices”

DO THIS: For schedulable care, that refusal runs against both the estimate duty and the transparency rules. Log who said it, and when, in those words. Then take the service to a provider who will price it — Step 2’s comparison is also your answer here. A seller who won’t name a price has told you something worth knowing before the service, not after.

If this happens: the quoted cash price changes at the register, or the bill arrives at sticker rates

DO THIS: Produce your paper: the dated quote, with the name of who quoted it, and the written agreement if you have one. Ask them to bill the agreed price, and put your disagreement in writing the same day (Step 5’s words) — paying the undisputed part, disputing the rest. A price agreed and then changed is not a misunderstanding to absorb; it is the deviation this lane’s folder exists to answer.

If this happens: a bill arrives from a provider nobody ever named

DO THIS: Don’t pay it on sight. Check it against your folder: were they on your estimate, your agreement, your list of separate billers? If they were never disclosed when you asked who else would bill you, say exactly that in writing and ask them to identify who ordered their service and where their price was disclosed to you before it. An undisclosed biller is a deviation (see the lists below), and the time to raise it is before payment, not after.

If this happens: a big hospital bill, and nobody ever mentioned financial assistance

DO THIS: Run the financial assistance door (above) before you pay anything — ask for the policy and the application, and note the account. A nonprofit hospital’s assistance policy that exists on paper and is never offered in person is a deviation, and your dated request is the paper that proves you walked through the door they didn’t open.

The mistakes — yours to not make

  1. Scheduling care without requesting the good-faith estimate in writing — then having nothing to dispute from when the bill doubles.
  2. Paying the chargemaster number — the sticker price — because it was the first and only number you were shown.
  3. Never asking for the cash or prompt-pay rate, because nobody mentioned one exists.
  4. Handing over a deposit without written answers on what it covers and how refunds work.
  5. Treating the facility’s price as the whole bill — forgetting the surgeon, anesthesiologist, lab, and imaging bills that arrive separately.
  6. Paying a lump-sum bill without demanding the itemized version and comparing it, line by line, to the estimate.
  7. (If you have a plan you’re bypassing) paying cash without checking whether the amount counts toward your deductible — and without the provider’s clear agreement to treat you as self-pay for this service.
  8. Assuming an emergency erased your rights — it postponed them; the itemized bill, the assistance screening, and the paper trail all still apply after.

The deviations — theirs to answer for

  1. The good-faith estimate never offered, offered only at check-in, or offered verbally when you asked for it in writing.
  2. A final bill $400 or more over the written estimate, per provider — the exact situation the federal dispute process exists for.
  3. No findable published prices, or a price file missing the discounted cash price the transparency rules require.
  4. “We don’t give prices” — for schedulable care, a refusal that runs against both the estimate duty and the transparency rules. Log who said it, and when.
  5. A quoted cash price that changes at the register, or a bill issued at chargemaster rates after a lower cash price was agreed.
  6. Financial assistance never mentioned by a nonprofit hospital to a self-pay patient facing a large bill — a policy that exists on paper and is never offered in person.
  7. Separate bills from providers never disclosed when you asked who else would bill you.

What “done” looks like for the cash-pay front end: for any care you can schedule, you have — before it happens — the estimate in writing, the published price checked, the cash rate asked for, and the agreed price on paper; and you have a folder where the bill, when it comes, will be compared against all four. You haven’t been treated yet, and the seller already knows this buyer reads.

Cash Care Price Worksheet

The Health Care Rule Kit · Cash-Pay Lane companion. One block per service or episode of care — print more copies as needed. Fill the top half BEFORE the service; the bottom half the day the bill arrives.

Your facts stay yours. Nothing on this page is uploaded, submitted, or sent anywhere. It exists in exactly one place — your hands.

Service — what care, in a few words, named the way the provider names it. So this page and their bill can be matched line to line later.

Provider / facility — who is providing and billing this part. One block per billing provider — the facility, the surgeon, the lab each get their own, because each sends its own estimate and its own bill.

Quoted cash price — the self-pay or prompt-pay rate you were given, with the date and the name of who quoted it. From Step 2. A quote with a name and a date is a position; without them, it’s a rumor.

Published price found — the discounted cash price from the provider’s published price list, with the date you found it. From Step 2. Write “not found” if the list didn’t have it — that’s information too.

Estimate received (date) — the date the written good-faith estimate reached you, and the amount. From Step 1. “Requested, never sent” is also an entry — write it, with the date you asked.

Agreed price in writing — the final agreed total, and where the writing lives (email, letter, portal message). From Step 3. If there’s no writing, this field says “none” — and you know your exposure before the service, not after.

Final bill — the amount of the itemized bill when it arrives. Itemized only — if it arrived as a lump sum, request the itemized version before filling this in.

Difference (bill minus estimate) — subtract. The number this whole worksheet exists to produce. At $400 or more over the estimate, for that provider, the federal dispute process opens — you have 120 calendar days from the date on the initial bill to start it.
