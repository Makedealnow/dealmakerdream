No insurance card — your price is agreed before the care, in writing.

  • Three questions tell you you’re in the right lane:

1. Do you pay for your care out of your own pocket — no insurance, no Medicare, no Medicaid, no plan paying the bills?

2. When a bill comes, does it come straight to you — the whole amount, with nobody else’s payment in between?

3. Have you ever been quoted one price and billed another — or been afraid to ask what something costs before saying yes?

Do you have Medicaid or a public program instead? Wrong lane — yours is stronger in several places. Go to the welfare lane.

If you said yes — especially to the first two — this is your lane. Your price is agreed before the care, in writing, and the paper you collect is what protects you after.

Katie’s rule for this stage

THE RULE: for scheduled, non-emergency care, you are owed a good-faith estimate of expected charges — in writing, before the care — and a final bill $400 or more above that estimate opens a federal dispute.

THE CHECK: the estimate, in hand, before the appointment — and the final bill set beside it, line by line.

THE MEANING: a price agreed in advance is a price that can be enforced. The estimate is your contract-shaped paper.

Step 1 — Before any appointment: the good-faith estimate

  • The good-faith estimate is a federal right (since 2022) for people paying for themselves — when you schedule care 3 or more business days ahead, or ask for an estimate directly.
  • What’s in it: the expected charges for the item or service, and for the items and services reasonably expected to go with it — every provider and facility involved, each one’s expected charges listed.
  • The timing: when you schedule at least 10 business days out, the estimate is due within 3 business days of scheduling; when you schedule 3–9 business days out, within 1 business day; when you simply ask without scheduling, within 3 business days of asking. (Verify current rules at cms.gov.)
  • One estimate is not the whole episode. If the hospital, the surgeon, and the lab each bill separately, the convening provider is supposed to coordinate one estimate covering the episode.
SAY THIS: “I’m paying for this myself — no insurance. Before I schedule, I need my good-faith estimate in writing, and I need it to cover everything reasonably expected with this care, including charges from any other provider or facility involved. If the total isn’t settled, I’m not scheduling yet.”

KEEP THIS PAPER: The estimate itself, with the date you received it. This is the single most important document in your lane.

Step 2 — Shop the price like a customer

DO THIS: You are the only payer in the room, which makes you the customer in a way insured patients never quite are. Get two or three prices for the same service, in writing, and compare totals — not just the headline fee.

SAY THIS: “I’m comparing prices for [the service] before I choose where to have it done. What is your total self-pay price — everything included — and can you put it in writing as my good-faith estimate?”

DO THIS: Ask for the self-pay or cash discount by name. Providers maintain prices for cash customers that are never the first number quoted. The discount exists whether or not it’s offered.

SAY THIS: “Is that your self-pay price, or your standard price? What is the cash price if I pay in full at the time of service?”

KEEP THIS PAPER: Every quote, with the office’s name, the date, and who gave it. The quote you don’t choose is still evidence of the market price.

Step 3 — Your doctor — two doors, same as everyone

Door 1 — you need to find one. Choose on the estimate and the cash price, not on habit. Get the new-patient visit’s price in writing before the appointment — and remember Step 1: schedule far enough ahead and the estimate right applies in full.

Door 2 — you already have one. The relationship doesn’t set the price; the paper does. At every visit from now on, the price conversation happens before anything beyond the visit itself is ordered — the visit’s own price, plus a price for anything added to it.

SAY THIS (at the doctor’s office, before tests are ordered): “I pay cash, so I need the price before anything is done. What will today’s visit cost, and if you’re ordering tests or referring me somewhere, what will each of those cost — and can I have those prices in writing before I agree to them?”

Tests and referrals multiply bills. In the insured lane, the desk play verifies the network. In your lane, the same play verifies the price — every facility, lab, and specialist the doctor sends you to gets its own estimate conversation before you go.

Step 4 — The visit — and what’s different at the desk

DO THIS: Announce your lane at check-in, every time. The desk’s default assumption is insurance. Say the words, and watch the paperwork change: no card photocopied, no plan billed — and your estimate right acknowledged.

SAY THIS: “I’m self-pay — I don’t have insurance, and I’ll be paying this myself. Please mark the account self-pay, and make sure my good-faith estimate is on file before I’m seen.”

Deposits: a deposit request is a negotiation, not a law. You can pay a stated deposit against a written estimate — that’s ordinary. What you don’t do is hand over open-ended payment against an unknown total.

The preventive-visit trap, cash edition: the visit you booked at one price becomes a different, larger bill when a problem gets discussed and treated. Before the visit, ask what makes the price change; during it, if the conversation turns to a new problem, it is fair — and expected, in your lane — to ask what addressing it today adds to the bill.

SAY THIS: “Before we go ahead with that today — what does adding it do to my bill? I’d rather know now than when the statement comes.”

Forms: the same clipboard discipline as everyone — read before signing, photograph what you sign. In your lane, the form to read twice is any financial agreement: it may commit you to “all charges” at prices never quoted. Your estimate and any written cash price go in your folder beside it.

Step 5 — After the care — the bill meets the estimate

DO THIS: When the bill arrives, before paying anything: demand the itemized bill if what arrived is a summary, then set it beside the estimate, line by line. Charges for things never done, quantities that don’t match, services never agreed to — circle them.

The $400 rule: if the final bill from a provider runs $400 or more above that provider’s good-faith estimate, you can open the federal patient-provider dispute resolution process — an independent review of the difference. The dispute must be started within 120 calendar days from the date on the initial bill, so write that deadline down the week a too-high bill arrives. Starting the process generally pauses collection on the disputed amount while it’s decided. (Verify the current process and deadlines at cms.gov.)

SAY THIS (first call on an over-estimate bill): “My good-faith estimate for this care was [amount], dated [date], and the bill is [amount] — that’s [difference] more. I need an itemized explanation of the difference, and I’m noting today’s date: I understand I have 120 days from the initial bill to open a federal dispute if we can’t resolve it. Please note the account that the charges are disputed.”

KEEP THIS PAPER: The estimate, the itemized bill, and your notes of every call — together, in the episode’s folder.

Step 6 — Financial assistance — ask even when you think you won’t qualify

DO THIS: Nonprofit hospitals are required to have a financial assistance policy — charity care and discounted care for patients under income limits the hospital sets — and to make it available. Ask for the policy and the application before care when it’s scheduled, and after the bill when it wasn’t.

SAY THIS: “I’m paying for this myself. Please send me your financial assistance policy and the application. I’d like to be screened before we talk about payment amounts.”

DO THIS: Ask at for-profit offices too. Sliding-scale fees, hardship discounts, and prompt-pay reductions exist outside the legal requirement — but only for patients who ask. The question costs nothing.

KEEP THIS PAPER: The policy, your application, and the answer — approved, reduced, or denied, it goes in the folder.

Emergencies — the one place the estimate can’t go first

Emergency care can’t wait for paperwork, and federal law (EMTALA) requires hospital emergency departments to screen and stabilize you regardless of ability to pay. There’s no estimate before an emergency — your lane’s protections there are the itemized bill afterward, the financial assistance application, and the negotiation. Get the care. The paper fight happens later, and this kit’s bill stage works for emergency bills the same way.

Step 7 — Your folder — the cash lane’s whole filing system

DO THIS: One folder — paper or phone photos — per episode of care: the estimate, every competing quote, the financial agreement you signed, the itemized bill, and your notes of every price conversation (who, when, what number). In a lane with no plan and no EOB, this folder is the entire record — and it’s enough.

If this happens

No estimate was ever offered

You asked, or you scheduled far enough ahead, and no estimate arrived before the care. That absence is itself the deviation: the estimate was owed. Document when you asked and what you were told, then dispute from that record — the bill stage’s letter adapts: a provider who never gave the required estimate is explaining two failures, not one.

If this happens

The bill arrives at triple the quote

A verbal quote isn’t the estimate — but if the number was given by the office and written down by you with a name and a date, it’s evidence of what was represented. Itemize, compare, and put the representation in the dispute letter. Going forward: nothing counts until it’s the written estimate.

If this happens

A deposit is demanded before emergency care

Emergency departments must screen and stabilize regardless of payment. If payment is demanded as a condition of emergency screening, that’s a deviation under federal law — note who said it and when, get the care, and report it afterward (the bill stage names the doors).

If this happens

You’re quoted the “standard price”

The first number is the rack rate almost nobody pays. The self-pay price, the cash discount, and the prompt-pay price are three separate asks — make all three, in that order, and get the best one into the written estimate.

If this happens

The office won’t put a price in writing

For scheduled care, the written estimate is your right, not a courtesy. An office that won’t write it down is telling you something about how the bill will go. That’s a reason to take Step 2’s competing quote and go elsewhere.

The mistakes — yours to not make

  1. Scheduling care without the estimate. The right exists; the protection only works if the paper does.
  2. Accepting the first price as the price. In this lane, the first number is a starting position.
  3. Comparing headline fees instead of totals. The facility fee, the lab, and the follow-up are part of the price.
  4. Signing the financial agreement unread — especially one that commits you to “all charges” with no estimate attached.
  5. Paying the summary bill. Itemized first, always — you can’t check a total, only lines.
  6. Letting the 120 days run. The dispute deadline counts from the initial bill, not from when you got around to it.
  7. Assuming you won’t qualify for assistance. The screening is the hospital’s job; asking is yours.

The deviations — theirs to answer for

  1. An estimate never provided for scheduled care, or provided only after the care happened.
  2. An estimate missing the episode — the facility’s charges listed, the surgeon’s and lab’s nowhere in it.
  3. A final bill $400+ over the estimate with no explanation offered.
  4. Charges for services never received, or quantities beyond what was done.
  5. The financial assistance policy never mentioned to a self-pay patient facing a large bill.
  6. Payment demanded before emergency screening.

What “done” looks like for this stage:

  • No care gets scheduled in your lane without the estimate in hand.
  • Every price you’ve agreed to exists in writing.
  • Every bill gets checked against its estimate before a dollar moves.

From here, your road joins everyone else’s. The shared stages ahead start with your doctor (Stage 3), then the visit itself (Stage 4), what happens after (Stage 5), and the bill itself (Stage 6) — written for all three lanes, with cash-lane notes wherever your rules differ.

Cash Care Price Worksheet

One page per episode of care — one procedure, one course of treatment, one problem being worked up. Start it the day you start collecting prices; finish it the day the last bill is settled. File it at the front of the episode’s papers.

Your facts stay yours. Nothing here is uploaded or sent anywhere — it exists in exactly one place: your hands.

Care needed — the service or procedure, in plain words, and who said you need it.

Estimate requested (date) — when you asked, and from whom. Your right runs from this date.

Estimate received (date) — when the written estimate reached you. If it never did, write that — the absence is evidence.

Estimate total — the full expected total, all providers and facilities included.
