Everything after the visit — mostly, you just wait. This stage is the watch.

  • When it runs smooth, the process moves on its own.
  • This kit is a watch, not a job — here for the moments that aren’t smooth.

The referral play — start here

  • The doctor says “I’m referring you to…” — that’s not one decision made. It’s the start of a new episode.
  • The gap: the referral is almost never prepared while you sit there. Staff prepare it after you leave — and “we’ll let you know” is where referrals die quietly.
  • The play has two halves: before you leave, three commitments and one piece of paper; during the window, three checks — destination, authorization, arrival.

What normally happens — the smooth path

The office prepares the referral, sends it, the other end receives it, the authorization lands, the appointment gets booked. When it runs smooth, you just wait. The checks below are how you know it’s still smooth.

Before you leave — the three commitments and the paper

  1. WHAT — exactly what is being referred, and to whom. Not chosen yet? Who chooses it, and when will you be told the name?
  2. WHO — the name of the person in this office who owns the referral, or the desk that does.
  3. WHEN — when you’ll hear it’s been sent. Set your own call date in your log.
SAY THIS: “Before I go, I need three things about this referral. First — what exactly is being referred, and to whom? If the doctor or facility hasn’t been chosen yet, who chooses them, and when will I be told the name? Second — who here handles the referral, so I know who to call? And third — when should I expect to hear that it’s been sent? If I haven’t heard by then, I’ll call and check.”

DO THIS: Write the three answers in your log before you leave the building.

KEEP THIS PAPER: The doctor’s order or the after-visit summary showing the referral was ordered. Photograph it into your album before you leave.

During the window — verify the destination yourself

DO THIS: Get the name if you don’t have it: “Has the referral been prepared yet, and who is it going to?” Then verify every name on the order — the specialist, the imaging facility, the lab — the Stage 3 way: office and plan, both, logged, before the appointment.

SAY THIS: “Which lab will my blood work be sent to? I need to check it’s in my plan before it’s drawn.”

The routing question: the same scan at an in-network independent imaging facility is frequently a fraction of the hospital department’s price.

SAY THIS: “I’d like this scan done at an in-network facility. Is there an independent imaging center in my plan you can send the order to instead of the hospital’s department? If the order has to name that facility, please write it that way.”
Lane note — insured: in an HMO or POS plan, the referral itself is also a coverage document — confirm it is on file before you go.
Lane note — welfare: your plan’s network and referral rules govern the destination the same way; a destination outside the network runs through the plan’s process, started early and kept in writing.
Lane note — cash: there is no network to verify — the verification is the price, in writing, for each destination.

Approvals — two different papers

  • A referral and an authorization are two different papers — a service can need both.

DO THIS: Before you leave, settle: does this need prior authorization or a formal referral on file? Who files it? By when — and how will you know it’s approved?

SAY THIS: “Does this need approval from my plan before the appointment — and who submits it, your office or the specialist’s? How will I know it’s approved?”

DO THIS: During the window, watch for it to appear — check your plan’s portal, or call the plan: “Has a referral or authorization for [service] with [destination] come through for me?”

KEEP THIS PAPER: The authorization number, what it covers, and who confirmed it — in your log.

During the window, last check — confirm the other end has it

DO THIS: Confirm with the receiving office that the referral arrived and is complete: “Has my referral from Dr. [primary] arrived, and is anything missing from it?” The referral is not real until the other end says they have it.

The three-check rule: the appointment does not happen until (1) the referral has been received at the other end, (2) the plan’s authorization is on file where your plan requires one, and (3) the destination is verified in-network. If any one is missing, the appointment waits while it gets fixed.

If this happens

The window passes with no word

Your log’s date arrives and nothing has come — so you call, in this order. First, the referring office: “On [date] I was told the referral to [destination] would be sent and I’d hear by [date]. I haven’t heard — has it gone, and to whom?” Then, if it stalls, the plan. Your dated log is the evidence that you did everything right.

Katie’s rule for this stage

THE RULE: federal law generally shields you from balance bills by providers you didn’t choose at an in-network facility.

THE CHECK: verify every name on every order — and never sign away the protection at check-in.

THE MEANING: the shield holds, and any such bill is a deviation, not a debt. Check it like a receipt: calmly, as a routine.

Step 1 — The referral loop, in full

  • A referral is not done when your doctor says it. It’s done when three facts are true: sent · received · booked. Track the three dates separately in your log.
  • The name on the referral is partly your choice. You can ask for a different specialist — closer, shorter wait, a hospital affiliation you prefer.
SAY THIS: “Before you send that — could the referral go to Dr. [name] instead? [She’s closer to me / his wait is shorter / she’s affiliated with the hospital I’d prefer.] If my plan needs the referral written a particular way for that, please let me know what it takes.”
SAY THIS (confirming arrival): “I’m following up on the referral from Dr. [primary] to Dr. [specialist], sent around [date]. Has it arrived, and can we book the appointment today? — and your name, please, for my records.”
  • Second opinions are a right, not an insult. Before a significant procedure or a life-altering diagnosis settles, you are generally entitled to another qualified doctor’s review.
Lane note — insured: most plans cover a second opinion from an in-network specialist; some require a referral or authorization. Check your Evidence of Coverage.
Lane note — welfare: Medicaid managed-care enrollees have a right to a second opinion from a qualified provider in the plan’s network — and if the plan has no qualified provider in network, the plan must arrange one outside it at no extra cost to you.
Lane note — cash: a second opinion is a purchase — get its price in writing before the visit.

KEEP THIS PAPER: Every referral, both verification logs for each specialist, second-opinion requests and answers — filed by episode in your care file.

And when you arrive at the specialist’s office, recognize the room: it is the desk again. Stage 4’s desk play runs there exactly as it ran at your primary doctor’s. New desk, same transaction.

Step 2 — Scheduled procedures: the cast of billers

A scheduled hospital or surgery-center procedure is not one service. It is a cast — and every member bills separately:

  • The facility — the hospital or ambulatory surgery center.
  • The surgeon or proceduralist
  • The anesthesiology group — usually a separate practice; historically the most common source of surprise bills.
  • Radiology — the reading physician’s group bills separately.
  • Pathology and lab — separate groups, separate bills.
  • Assistants and others — assistant surgeon, consulting physician, device or monitoring company, equipment suppliers.
  • Each cast member has its own network status, its own price, its own bill. “The hospital is in-network” answers exactly one of those questions.

DO THIS: Get the cast list in writing before the day. Then work the list: for each name, the Step 1 verification — or, cash lane, the price.

SAY THIS: “Before the procedure on [date], I need the complete list of everyone who will bill me separately — the facility, Dr. [surgeon], the anesthesia group, radiology, pathology and lab, and any assistants. Please send it to me in writing. I’ll be confirming each one’s [network status / price] before the day.”

KEEP THIS PAPER: The cast list itself, with a checked-off verification or price beside every name.

Authorization in hand before the day — get the authorization number and its scope in writing before the day.

Lane note — insured: most scheduled procedures need prior authorization, covering the procedure, the facility, and the date window.
Lane note — welfare: confirm the authorization exists, by number, before the day.
Lane note — cash: your authorization-equivalent is the good-faith estimate, per cast member, in writing, before the day.

Financial assistance: ask before, not after.

SAY THIS: “If cost is going to be a problem for this procedure — and it may be — I want to apply under the hospital’s financial assistance policy before the date, not after. Please send me the policy and the application, and tell me the deadline to have it decided before [procedure date].”

Step 3 — No Surprises, in full

The federal No Surprises Act (in effect since 2022) — what it actually does for an insured patient:

  • Emergency care: out-of-network emergency services are cost-shared as if in-network — and the provider may not bill you the balance above that.
  • Ancillary providers at an in-network facility: the specialists you don’t choose — anesthesiology, radiology, pathology, laboratory, assistant surgeons — may not balance-bill you when they’re out-of-network but the facility is in-network. Your cost is the in-network cost-sharing; the plan and provider settle the rest.
  • Out-of-network clinicians at an in-network facility generally: the same principle — your episode at an in-network building is priced to you at in-network cost-sharing.

A balance bill from an out-of-network ancillary provider for care at an in-network facility is a deviation — Stage 6 gives you the dispute it deserves. (Verify the current rules: the federal No Surprises help desk and your plan can confirm how they apply to your plan type.)

Lane note — welfare: your billing shield is stronger and older than this one: a participating provider accepts the program’s payment as payment in full beyond any allowed cost-sharing.
Lane note — cash: no network shield in your lane; your protection is the good-faith estimate and the agreed price in writing, per destination.

If this happens — the waiver trap

A form asks you to sign away the protection

For certain non-emergency services, an out-of-network provider may ask you to sign a notice and consent form — waiving your surprise-billing protections and agreeing to out-of-network rates.

  • It is optional. Signing is a choice, not a condition the law imposes.
  • One practical consequence: for a planned, non-emergency service with an out-of-network doctor, declining a valid form can mean the office reschedules instead of treating you that day. That is why a valid form is supposed to be in your hands well before the appointment — one that first appears at check-in, inside a stack of forms, is not how the rule is written.
  • It is never valid for emergency care. No consent form can waive your protections for emergency services.
  • It is never valid for the ancillary cast. Anesthesiology, radiology, pathology, lab, assistant surgeons, and other providers you don’t choose at an in-network facility cannot ask you to waive — the exception does not exist for them.
  • It applies only to the provider named on it.
SAY THIS: “This paper asks me to waive my surprise-billing protections. I’m not signing it at check-in. I’ll take it home, review it, and call my plan.”
SAY THIS (full): “I understand this form asks me to waive my federal surprise-billing protections and agree to out-of-network charges. I’m not signing it today. I’ll review it and call my plan, and if I decide to sign, I’ll return it signed before the deadline. Please note that I was given the form today, [date].”

KEEP THIS PAPER: The form itself — signed or not — with the date and hour it was handed to you written on your copy.

Step 4 — Emergency care and the hospital

EMTALA — the screening right

  • Under the federal Emergency Medical Treatment and Labor Act, anyone who comes to a hospital emergency department is entitled to a medical screening examination, and, if an emergency condition exists, to stabilizing treatment or an appropriate transfer.
  • Ability to pay is not part of the test. Registration is fine; delay for payment is not — the hospital may not delay screening to ask about payment, demand a deposit before screening or stabilization, or route you through insurance authorization while an emergency waits.
SAY THIS: “I understand you’ll need my information, and I’ll provide it. I also understand I’m entitled to a medical screening exam and stabilizing treatment regardless of payment, and I’d like the exam not to be delayed for insurance or payment questions.”

KEEP THIS PAPER: Afterward: the ER records and discharge papers, names and times, and any payment conversation that happened before screening, noted with the time.

Lane note — insured: emergency services are billed to you at in-network cost-sharing even at an out-of-network facility, and no notice-and-consent form can waive any of it.
Lane note — cash: no good-faith estimate for a true emergency. What you still hold: EMTALA, the hospital’s financial assistance policy, and the itemized-bill rights Stage 6 develops.

Step 5 — The hospital stay and discharge

  • Everything the hospital hands you, you keep. Everything you sign, you hold a copy of.
  • Discharge is a plan, and you are a party to it. The hospital must assess your post-hospital needs, build the plan with you, and offer you a choice of post-acute providers — noting which ones its own organization owns or has a financial interest in.
SAY THIS: “Before we settle the discharge plan, I need the list of [home health agencies / rehabilitation facilities / nursing facilities] that serve my area and accept [my plan / my program], including any the hospital has a financial interest in — that has to be disclosed. We’ll choose from the list, and I’d like our choice noted in the plan.”

KEEP THIS PAPER: The discharge plan, the provider list, your discharge instructions and medication list, and the names of discharge staff.

If this happens — Medicare discharge

The same-day Medicare discharge appeal

  • Medicare patients receive a notice titled “An Important Message from Medicare” — it explains your right to appeal a discharge you believe is too soon, to an independent Quality Improvement Organization (QIO) named on the notice.
  • It is expedited: act the same day — the operative deadline is generally no later than the day of discharge.
  • It is the most time-sensitive document in this entire kit — read it the hour it arrives; if you dispute the discharge, make the call that day.

KEEP THIS PAPER: The Important Message notice itself, the date and time it was handed to you (write it on the notice), and the log of any QIO call.

DO THIS: Leave the hospital with four things: the follow-up appointment (date and time), the aftercare start (who, when), the equipment supplier’s name and paperwork, and the complete stack of the stay’s paper.

The mistakes — yours to not make

  1. Treating a referral as done when your doctor said it — never confirming it was sent, received, and booked.
  2. Accepting the specialist named on the referral as an assignment, when asking for a different name was a thirty-second conversation.
  3. Verifying the hospital is in-network and assuming the whole episode is — while five other cast members bill under five other statuses.
  4. Scheduling a procedure without the cast list in writing, every status or price checked, before the day.
  5. Letting “we handle the authorization” substitute for an authorization number you hold yourself.
  6. Waiting until the bill arrives to ask about financial assistance that could have been decided before the procedure.
  7. Signing a notice-and-consent waiver inside a stack of forms because the pen was moving.
  8. Believing a payment conversation may lawfully come before an emergency screening exam — and not noting the time if one does.
  9. Letting discharge planning happen to you: one facility named, no list offered, no choice recorded.
  10. Filing the “Important Message from Medicare” to read later. Later, for that notice, is a different and worse appeal.
  11. Leaving the hospital with the follow-up “to be arranged” and no date.
  12. Losing track of the equipment supplier’s name, while its monthly billing continues past the equipment’s return.

The deviations — theirs to answer for

  1. A referral documented in the visit and never transmitted — or sent to a specialist your plan cannot cover, without anyone saying so.
  2. A cast member’s out-of-network status known to the facility and never disclosed before a scheduled procedure.
  3. A balance bill from an out-of-network ancillary provider for care at an in-network facility: the federal protection’s core case.
  4. A notice-and-consent waiver presented for emergency care, or by an ancillary provider not permitted to ask for one.
  5. A waiver presented for the first time on the day of the procedure, under time pressure.
  6. Payment demanded, or screening delayed for insurance processing, before a medical screening examination.
  7. Discharge executed without a provider list, without disclosing the hospital’s financial interest, or over your stated choice without explanation.
  8. A Medicare discharge pushed forward while the patient’s Important Message rights go unmentioned.
  9. Aftercare billed by suppliers you were never given the name of before the service began.

What “done” looks like for Stage 5:

  • Every referral is sent, received, and booked — or its failure is documented and being chased.
  • Every scheduled procedure ran off a cast list you held in advance.
  • You can recognize a notice-and-consent waiver on sight. You know the emergency rule: screening first, payment never a precondition.
  • You left any hospital stay holding the discharge plan, the provider list, the follow-up date, the equipment paperwork, and every notice.

Care Episode Planner — the worksheet

One page per episode — one procedure, one hospital stay, one course of aftercare. Start it the day the episode is scheduled; finish it the day you’re home. File it at the front of the episode’s papers.

Your facts stay yours. Nothing here is uploaded or sent anywhere — it exists in exactly one place: your hands.

Episode / procedure — what is happening, in plain words, and who ordered or referred it.

Date(s) — the scheduled date, and the actual date if it moves. A moved date can quietly void authorizations and estimates.

Facility — the hospital or surgery center’s name and location.

The cast list — status checked — every provider or group expected to bill you, one line each, with its box: in-network / program-participating confirmed (insured and welfare lanes) or price/estimate in hand (cash lane). No box stays empty on the day of the procedure.

Cast — Surgeon (status checked ☐)

Cast — Anesthesiology group (status checked ☐)

Cast — Radiology (status checked ☐)

Cast — Pathology / Lab (status checked ☐)

Cast — Assistants / others (status checked ☐)

Authorization # — the number, what it covers (procedure, facility, dates), and who confirmed it. “The office is handling it” is not an entry — the number is.

Estimates received — the plan’s cost estimate (insured), the good-faith estimates per provider (cash), or coverage-and-authorization confirmation (welfare), with dates. Stage 6 compares every bill against this field first.

Financial assistance applied? — yes/no, the date applied, and the answer or pending status.

Discharge papers checklist — check as received: discharge plan · provider list offered · discharge instructions + medication list · follow-up appointment booked (date) · aftercare start confirmed · equipment supplier named + delivery papers · Important Message from Medicare received (Medicare stays; note the time).

Print this and keep it at the front of the episode’s papers in your care file.
