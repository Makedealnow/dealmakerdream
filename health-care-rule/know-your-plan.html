Know what kind of plan you have, what's covered, and what isn't — before anyone bills you.

Do this in one calm sitting, with your insurance card and your plan papers in front of you. Nothing here needs you to be sick or to have a bill. That's the point.

The steps

Step 1 — Name your plan kind

DO THIS: Find the letters on your card — HMO, PPO, EPO, POS, or high-deductible — and note your kind. It's the first line of your plan card (on paper, the first line of the worksheet below).

KEEP THIS PAPER: Your card itself — keep it where you can reach it.

Step 2 — Get the two documents that answer everything

DO THIS: Get your Summary of Benefits and Coverage (the short one — look here first) and your Evidence of Coverage (the full contract). They're in your plan's app or member website, or one phone call away.

SAY THIS: "Please send me my Summary of Benefits and Coverage and my Evidence of Coverage — the full plan document."

KEEP THIS PAPER: Both documents, in one folder you can find in one minute.

Step 3 — Learn the money words

DO THIS: Pull six numbers out of your Summary — these are your money words: your deductible, your out-of-pocket maximum (your ceiling for the year — the most protective number you own), what a regular doctor visit costs you, what a specialist costs you, what an emergency room visit costs you, and how your prescriptions are priced. In Question 1 below, our lookup shows them in one table — save that table as a photo on your phone; that photo is your plan card. Prefer paper? The worksheet at the bottom of this page is the same six numbers, ready to print and write in.

Step 4 — Check a service before you get it

DO THIS: Before any test, scan, or procedure that isn't routine, look it up — then call the number on the back of your card and ask.

SAY THIS: "I'm scheduled for this service on this date. Is it covered? Is the provider in my network? Does it need approval first? And what will my share be?"

KEEP THIS PAPER: Your note of that call — the date, the name of the person, the reference number, and the answer. A logged answer is evidence.

Step 5 — Check the network twice

DO THIS: For your main doctor and any specialist, check twice: once in the plan's list of doctors, and once by calling the doctor's office and asking if they take your exact plan. Lists of doctors are wrong more often than you'd think — the office knows.

KEEP THIS PAPER: A dated picture or printout of the listing you relied on.

Step 6 — Ask about approval first

DO THIS: Some care needs the plan's OK before it happens. For anything beyond a regular office visit, ask: "Does this need approval first?" If yes, make sure the approval is in place before the appointment — and write down who told you, and when.

Where did you get your plan?

Your plan comes through a job — here's the honest answer

Employer plans aren't published in any government file — not ours, not anyone's. No website can look yours up, and any site that claims it can is guessing. But you don't need a lookup: you need your plan's two documents, and you are entitled to both.

DO THIS: Call the member number on the back of your insurance card — or ask your HR department — and say this:

SAY THIS: "Please send me my Summary of Benefits and Coverage and my Evidence of Coverage — the full plan document."

KEEP THIS PAPER: Both documents, in one folder you can find in one minute. Write down who you talked to, and when.

When the documents arrive, your money words are in the Summary's cost tables — photograph those pages and keep the photo as your plan card. If a number in the photo ever disagrees with your plan's own document, the document wins. (The worksheet below lists the same six numbers, if you prefer paper.) Question 2 — the Next question button, below — works from those pages with you.

Medicaid works differently — and this kit has a whole lane for it

Medicaid is run state by state, and its plans aren't in the federal files this lookup uses — so there's no honest way to look yours up here. What protects you in Medicaid isn't a price table; it's your rights: written notice before anything changes, a fair hearing if you disagree, and coverage that continues while you appeal.

DO THIS: Keep every letter your state sends you, and note the name of your plan exactly as it appears on your Medicaid card. Your state's Medicaid agency is the official source for what your plan covers.

The kit's welfare lane — Stay on the Track, Keep Your Rights — walks your whole road from there.

The question she copies is the exact one in the box below — with those pages pasted under its last line. Here is what sends back:

Sample answer

1. Is it covered? Covered only under conditions. The document's own words: "Imaging (CT/PET scans, MRIs) — 30% coinsurance after deductible," and "Imaging needs a referral from your primary care doctor." In plain language: your plan covers the MRI, but only with a referral from your regular doctor.

2. The conditions: A referral is required — from your primary care doctor. An in-network provider is required: "Care from out-of-network providers is not covered, except in an emergency." Prior authorization required? Not in the document — the pasted pages never mention it.

3. Your cost: 30% coinsurance after your deductible. How much the deductible is: Not in the document — these pages don't state it, so no dollar total can be worked out from them.

4. Questions the document does NOT answer: Does the plan have to approve this MRI before March 12? How much of the deductible is already met? Is Valley Imaging Center in the plan's network? (The document never names providers.)
