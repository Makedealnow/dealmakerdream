Find your doctor — or check the one you already have. Most of the time it’s a few calm minutes of checking, done once. The kit is here for the day a listing turns out wrong.

Up to now, your lane had its own front end — your plan, your program, your prices. This stage is where every lane merges onto the same road. And the first stop on that road is a person: your primary doctor. Not a name on a list. A doctor who knows you before you’re sick — because that doctor is your gatekeeper. Referrals start there. Authorizations run through there. And the hospital you’re sent to, someday, will very likely be your doctor’s hospital.

Do this stage in calm, before you’re sick, like every front-end stage before it. A primary doctor chosen in a hurry, from a hospital bed or a pain spike, is a stranger. Chosen here, they’re yours.

Door 2 — I already have a doctor

You have a doctor you like, and you’re keeping them. Good — this kit never asks you to shop for a doctor you’re happy with. But “I have a doctor” is a claim about the past, and networks churn quietly. A doctor who was in your plan last year may be out of it this year. A doctor who moved practices may have changed network status with the address — participation often follows the practice, not the person. A practice may have quietly stopped taking your plan’s product while still taking the insurer’s other plans. And offices almost never volunteer any of it: you find out at a desk, mid-illness, which is the worst classroom in this kit. So Door 2 is short, and it is not optional: you don’t re-shop for your doctor — you verify the one you have.

DO THIS — the whole path, three checks:

  1. Verify twice, for this plan year — the same two independent confirmations Step 1 teaches, run on the doctor you already have: the office (Step 2’s questions work — ask them about your existing relationship, not a new one) and your plan or agency, confirming this doctor for you under your exact plan or program. Last year’s yes is not this year’s.
  2. Confirm the office will book you under your exact plan — by its full name, at the location you actually visit. “We still take your insurance” is not the same answer as “you are in-network for [your plan product], here, now.”
  3. Log both confirmations next to your plan card — date, name of who confirmed, what they confirmed. Door 2’s paper is small. It is also the paper that answers Stage 4’s desk if the desk disagrees with it.

KEEP THIS PAPER: Your log of both confirmations, next to your plan card.

Then skip ahead: Step 4 (your records) and Step 5 (your care file) below apply to you exactly as written. Steps 1–3 are the finding path; read them only if a Door 2 check fails. A failed check means you are standing at Door 1 now — and the stage is there for you.

Lane note — welfare (Door 2): your plan keeps its own answer to “who is your doctor?” — and it may not be the doctor you actually see. Auto-assignment, a move, or an office change can leave the plan showing a name you’ve never visited, while the doctor you use isn’t the one the plan counts. Check who the plan says your primary doctor is (the member line or your portal). If it’s wrong — or the named doctor is unreachable, far away, or no longer practicing — change the assignment to the doctor you actually use, using the change rights in Step 1’s welfare note, and confirm the change has taken effect before you rely on it. In this lane, referrals and authorizations run through the doctor the plan has on file; a mismatch there is a denial waiting for a date.
Lane note — cash (Door 2): your whole check is one question: does the office’s cash price for a visit still hold? Cash prices drift the way networks do. Confirm the current visit price — and what it includes — before your next appointment, and log it with the date, the way the cash lane taught.

Katie’s rule for this stage

THE RULE: the list a plan or program publishes — its directory of doctors — has to be accurate. That’s a regulated obligation, and it exists because the lists are so often wrong. And an office has to bill under the contracts it actually holds: “we take your insurer” is not the same thing as “we are in-network for your exact plan.”

THE CHECK: verify twice, before you rely on any name. The office confirms it, and the plan or agency confirms it — two independent yeses, with dates, in your log, plus a dated picture of the listing you relied on.

THE MEANING: if the listing turns out wrong, your dated picture and your log are the protection — a doctor you chose because a directory said “in-network and accepting patients,” on a date you can prove, is a reliance the system has to answer for. Check it the way you’d check a receipt: calmly, as a routine. The rule is named here, before you need it, so a violation reads as a paperwork fact — not a confrontation.

The steps — Door 1

Step 1 — Choosing: start from your lane’s list, then verify twice

DO THIS: Every lane gives you a list to start from. None of the lists can be trusted as printed. So the rule of this step, for every lane, is the same: the list nominates; the verification decides. Before any name goes on your worksheet as your doctor, make two independent confirmations: (1) the office — call the practice itself and ask the questions in Step 2; (2) the plan or agency — confirm with your insurer, your Medicaid managed-care plan, or your state agency that this doctor is listed for you under your exact plan or program. (Cash lane: there is no second party — your second verification is the written price confirmation in Step 2.) When the two answers agree, you have a doctor. When they disagree, believe the disagreement, not either answer — and resolve it before you rely on the doctor, in writing where you can.

Lane note — insured: start from your plan’s provider directory (Stage 1, Step 5). If your plan kind requires you to name a primary care physician — many HMO and POS plans do — that naming is a formal step with the plan, not just a choice you make in your head: the doctor you named is the one whose referrals and visits the plan counts. Verify the doctor is in-network for your exact plan, at the location you’ll actually visit, and complete the plan’s PCP-selection step if your plan has one.
Lane note — welfare: if your managed-care plan assigned you a primary doctor — auto-assignment is common when you enroll and don’t choose — that assignment is a default, not a destiny. In most programs you may change your assigned doctor, and often your plan, within windows and rules your state and plan publish. Read the assignment letter as what it is: the system choosing for you because you hadn’t yet. Keep that letter — it is the paper that proves who was assigned, when, and what your change rights are. And do not drift without a doctor because the assignment looked official or because the assigned office is far away: an assigned doctor you’ve never seen is the welfare lane’s version of having no doctor at all.
Lane note — cash: there is no directory. Your list is the practices near you, recommendations, and your own search — and your verification is about price as much as participation (Step 2’s script covers both). Some practices offer membership or direct-primary-care arrangements — a recurring fee covering primary-care access outside insurance entirely. This kit names the model neutrally and makes no recommendation: if you meet it, get its terms in writing — what the fee covers, what it excludes, how hospital and specialist care are handled (almost always: separately) — and judge it with the cash lane’s rules, like any other price.

KEEP THIS PAPER: The directory or assignment listing you relied on — screenshot or printout, dated — plus your log of both verification calls: date, name of who you spoke to, and what they confirmed.

One warning travels forward: even after both checks say yes, the desk can still say no on the day — and the desk play at the start of Stage 4 covers exactly that moment.

Step 2 — The first call: what to ask the office

DO THIS: One call does most of this stage’s work. Make it before you book anything, and log it like every call in this kit: date, name, answers.

SAY THIS: “Hello, I’m choosing a primary care doctor and I’m calling to confirm a few things before I schedule. One: is Dr. [name] accepting new patients right now? Two: does the practice take [my exact plan name / my Medicaid managed-care plan / — cash lane: — and if so, what are your new-patient and regular visit prices for someone paying cash]? Three: when is the first available appointment for a new patient? Four: which hospital is Dr. [name] affiliated with — where would I be sent if I needed hospital care?”

Accepting new patients — the single most common directory lie, in every lane. A listing is a snapshot; this answer is today.

Participation, in the plan’s exact name — practices bill under contracts with specific plans and products. “We take [insurer]” is not the same answer as “we are in-network for [your plan product].” Ask with the full name from your card.

First available appointment — a doctor you can’t see for four months is a doctor you don’t have, whatever the directory says. If the wait is unreasonable, that is information: choose someone reachable, and note the wait in your log.

Hospital affiliation — ask it now, in calm, because it decides something later under pressure: your doctor’s hospital is, in practice, very likely to be your hospital. You are not just choosing a doctor in this call. You are choosing the building.

DO THIS: Make this call for your first-choice doctor. If any answer fails — not accepting, doesn’t take your plan or program, unreachable wait — move to the next name on the list and make the same call. Do not book an establish-care visit (Step 3) until one office passes all four.

KEEP THIS PAPER: Your log of the call — and of any failed calls before it. The failures are documentation of the directory problem, dated, in your hand.

Step 3 — Getting on board: the establish-care visit

DO THIS: Most practices will not treat you as their patient for a sick visit, a referral, or an after-hours call until you have had a first, get-established appointment — often called an establish-care or new-patient visit. Being established before you’re sick is the entire point of doing it now: the first visit as a known patient is a different experience from the first visit as a stranger in pain.

DO THIS: Book the establish-care visit once Step 2’s call passes, and bring: your photo ID; your insurance or program card (or, cash lane, your agreed price confirmation); a list of your current medications — names and doses, from the bottles, not from memory; the names and locations of your previous doctors and any hospital you’ve used (Step 4 needs the same list, so write it once, carefully); and your worksheet.

SAY THIS (at booking): “I want to schedule a new-patient, establish-care visit with Dr. [name]. I’m bringing my medication list and my previous providers’ information so you can request my records.”

KEEP THIS PAPER: The visit summary the practice gives you, and the names of everyone you dealt with. This visit is the first entry in your care file (Step 5).

Lane note — insured: confirm at booking whether the establish-care visit is billed as a preventive visit or a regular office visit under your plan — the two can price very differently against your deductible and copays — and log the answer. If your plan required a formal PCP selection (Step 1), confirm the selection is on file with the plan, not just the office, before this visit.
Lane note — welfare: if you changed an auto-assigned doctor (Step 1), confirm the change has taken effect — with the plan and with the new office — before booking, and keep the change confirmation with the assignment letter. An establish-care visit booked under a doctor your plan still shows as someone else is paperwork trouble you can prevent with one call.
Lane note — cash: confirm the establish-care visit’s cash price in writing before the appointment, the way the cash lane taught: quoted price, what’s included (are any labs or tests part of it, or billed separately?), and who quoted it, in your log.

The steps — both doors end here

Step 4 — Moving your records: they are yours

DO THIS: Your new primary doctor works better with your history — and your history belongs, in the ways that matter here, to you. Under the federal HIPAA right of access, you have the right to get a copy of your medical records from your providers, and to direct that a copy be sent to a third party you choose — including your new doctor. This is not a courtesy the old office extends. It is a right you exercise, and this stage is where you exercise it on purpose, early, while nothing is urgent.

DO THIS: Providers may require the request in writing and may have their own form — ask for the form, or send a letter. Either way, the request that works has the same contents: who you are, exactly which records, the date range, where to send them, and your signature and date.

SAY THIS (letter or form cover note): “[Your name, date of birth, address, phone]. Under my right of access to my own health records, I am requesting a copy of my medical records for [date range, or: my complete record] — including visit notes, test and imaging results, and my medication history — to be sent to [new doctor’s name, practice, address / fax / portal]. [Or: and I will pick up / please send to me directly.] Please let me know the cost before you process this, and if you need anything further from me to complete it. — [Signature, date.]”

DO THIS: Send the request in a way that proves the date — portal message with a screenshot, fax with a confirmation page, or mail with proof of mailing — and log it. Ask each prior provider who holds a meaningful piece of your history: previous primary doctors first, then specialists and hospitals with records your new doctor will actually need.

The clock, stated carefully: federal HIPAA rules require a covered provider to act on an access request within a set outer window — commonly 30 days from the request, with a limited extension the provider must explain in writing — and many states set a faster deadline or stricter rules of their own. Treat the federal window as the floor of your rights, not the whole of them: verify your state’s rule (your state health department or your new provider’s office can point you to it), and write the date your request is due on your log the day you send it.

What it may cost: HIPAA limits the fee for your own records requested for yourself to a reasonable, cost-based fee — copying and labor, not a ransom — and electronic records requested electronically are generally cheaper still; some states cap fees lower. If a quote sounds like a wall rather than a fee, ask for it in writing, itemized: “Please send me the fee in writing, with what it covers.” A fee that can’t be itemized can be questioned.

When records are slow-walked — the deviation, and the route: delay past the window, silence, or a demand for fees the rules don’t support is a deviation, and it has a referee: complaints about a provider’s handling of your access right go to the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services, Office for Civil Rights (OCR) — the federal office that enforces HIPAA. Filing is free, done in writing or online, and it runs on your paper: the dated request, your log of follow-ups, the provider’s responses. One call first usually suffices:

SAY THIS: “My request went in on [date]. I know there’s a deadline for acting on it, and I’d like to complete this without a complaint. Can you tell me today when my records will be sent?”

KEEP THIS PAPER: A copy of every records request, proof of its date, the due date you calculated, and every reply. Filed in your care file (Step 5), this bundle is the complete story if OCR ever reads it — and the complete reassurance that your history actually moved.

Step 5 — Your care file starts here

DO THIS: Every lane’s front end started a folder. This stage starts the folder the rest of the road runs on — your care file. One place, paper or digital, holding the human map of your care: who your providers are, where your records live, and (from Stage 4 onward) your log of visits, calls, and papers. Start it today with three things: your My Care Team worksheet (below), the verification notes and establish-care summary from Steps 1–3, and the records bundle from Step 4. Everything after this stage — every referral, authorization, hospital stay, and bill in Stages 4–6 — gets filed behind it, in order. The care file is why, in Stage 6, you will be comparing their bill against your record instead of your memory.

DO THIS: Fill in the My Care Team worksheet in one sitting, from the papers this stage produced. If a line is blank — a records location you don’t know, a hospital affiliation you didn’t ask — the blank is a to-do, not a failure. Close the blanks within the week.

Every year, run it again

DO THIS: Every new plan year, the verification runs again — even if nothing in your life changed. Plans rebuild their networks; doctors change practices, contracts, and panels; and none of them mail you a notice that your doctor quietly fell out of yours. One short check each year — the two confirmations, logged — is the cheapest step in this entire kit, and it is the step that prevents the worst branch of what comes next: arriving sick, card in hand, and hearing “we don’t take that anymore.”

KEEP THIS PAPER: The dated log of each year’s check, next to your plan card.

The mistakes — yours to not make

  1. Picking a doctor from a directory, a list, or an assignment letter and never verifying — then meeting the truth during an illness.
  2. Verifying with only one party. The office and the plan (or agency) keep separate files; one confirmation is half a fact.
  3. Never asking the hospital affiliation — and discovering, at the worst moment, which building your choice of doctor chose for you.
  4. Drifting without a primary doctor because the assigned one looked official, or the search felt like homework. An unchosen doctor is the same as none.
  5. Booking sick care at a practice where you were never established — and getting the stranger’s version of the first visit.
  6. Assuming your records followed you. They move when you move them, in writing, on a clock you track.
  7. Asking for records verbally, keeping no dated copy of the request, and having nothing to show when the window passes.
  8. Starting no care file — leaving the worksheets, summaries, and logs scattered, so Stage 6’s fight runs on memory.

The deviations — theirs to answer for

  1. Directory or assignment listings naming doctors who are not accepting patients, not participating, or no longer at the listed practice — in every lane, a known and regulated accuracy problem.
  2. An office confirming participation in an insurer generally while not in-network for your specific plan product — a distinction the office’s billing staff knows and the patient pays for.
  3. An auto-assignment treated by a plan as if it were your informed choice, with your change rights nowhere mentioned in the letter that announced it.
  4. Records requests ignored past the federal window (or your state’s faster one), with no written explanation of any extension.
  5. Record fees quoted as flat, unexplained sums, rather than the reasonable, cost-based fee the access right allows — especially fees that shrink when itemization is requested.
  6. An establish-care visit billed under a different visit type than the one described at booking, changing what you owe without anyone saying so.

What “done” looks like for Stage 3: you can name your primary doctor, their practice, and their hospital without looking it up — and then you look it up anyway, on your worksheet, because it’s written there. The doctor was verified twice, with dated notes. You’re established, or the visit is booked. Your records are requested, with due dates on your log. And your care file exists, with this stage’s paper in it, waiting for Stage 4. The shared road is open — and you’re on it with a home base.

My Care Team — the worksheet

Your facts stay yours. Nothing on this page is uploaded, submitted, or sent anywhere. It exists in exactly one place — your hands. Fill it in one sitting from this stage’s papers; update it the day anything on it changes. Keep it at the front of your care file.

My lane + plan / program — insured, cash-pay, or welfare, with the plan or program’s exact name. So every person helping you — and every later worksheet — starts from the right rulebook.

Member / ID number — your number under that plan or program, copied from the card. Offices ask for it on every call in this stage; having it here ends the wallet search.

Primary doctor — the doctor you chose and verified, full name as the practice uses it. Not the assigned or listed name — the verified one, from Step 2’s call.

Practice name + address — the office you’ll actually visit. Doctors practice at more than one address, and participation and affiliation can differ by location.

Practice phone — the office’s number, including any separate scheduling or nurse line.

Patient portal — the portal name or web address, and where your login is kept (not the password itself). Visit summaries, test results, and messages land here — and portal messages are dated writing, which this kit values.

Established? (date) — the date of your establish-care visit, or the date it’s booked for. Before this date, you’re a stranger to the practice; after it, you’re their patient. The date is the line.

Verified with plan / agency (date) — when your insurer, plan, or agency confirmed this doctor for you, and who confirmed it. The second verification from Step 1 — half of your directory protection.

Hospital affiliation — the hospital your primary doctor is affiliated with. Probably your hospital, if it ever matters. Better learned here than in an ambulance.

Specialists I use — name, specialty, practice phone, one line each.

Pharmacy — name, location, phone.

Records: where my history lives — previous doctors, hospitals, and clinics holding your records, one line each, with “requested (date) / received (date)” beside each. A blank “received” line past its due date is a follow-up already written for you.

Records now held by my primary doctor — what has arrived, checked off as it lands.

Print this and keep it at the front of your care file.
