Door 2 — I already have a doctor

  • You don't re-shop for your doctor — you verify the one you have.
  • Networks churn quietly: a doctor in your plan last year may be out this year — and offices almost never volunteer it.
  • You'd find out at a desk, mid-illness. Three checks now prevent that.

DO THIS — the whole path, three checks:

  1. Verify twice, for this plan year — the office and your plan or agency both confirm this doctor, under your exact plan. Last year’s yes is not this year’s.
  2. Confirm the office will book you under your exact plan — by its full name, at the location you actually visit.
  3. Log both confirmations next to your plan card — date, name of who confirmed, what they confirmed.

KEEP THIS PAPER: Your log of both confirmations, next to your plan card.

Then skip ahead: Step 4 (your records) and Step 5 (your care file) below apply to you exactly as written. Read Steps 1–3 only if a check fails — a failed check means you're at Door 1 now.

Lane note — welfare (Door 2): check who the plan says your primary doctor is — auto-assignment or a move can leave it showing a name you've never visited. If it's wrong, change the assignment to the doctor you actually use and confirm the change took effect. Referrals run through the doctor the plan has on file.
Lane note — cash (Door 2): one question: does the office’s cash price for a visit still hold? Confirm the current price — and what it includes — before your next appointment, and log it with the date.

Katie’s rule for this stage

THE RULE: the plan’s published doctor list has to be accurate — that’s a regulated obligation. And “we take your insurer” is not the same as “we are in-network for your exact plan.”

THE CHECK: verify twice, before you rely on any name — the office confirms it, and the plan or agency confirms it. Two yeses, with dates, in your log, plus a dated picture of the listing.

THE MEANING: if the listing turns out wrong, your dated picture and log are the protection. Check it like a receipt: calmly, as a routine.

The steps — Door 1

Step 1 — Choosing: the list nominates; the verification decides

DO THIS: Start from your lane’s list — then make two independent confirmations before any name becomes your doctor:

  • (1) The office — call the practice; ask Step 2’s questions.
  • (2) The plan or agency — confirm this doctor is listed for you under your exact plan or program.
  • Cash lane: no second party — your second verification is the written price confirmation in Step 2.
  • If the two answers disagree, believe the disagreement — resolve it before you rely on the doctor.
Lane note — insured: start from your plan’s provider directory (Stage 1, Step 5). If your plan kind requires naming a primary care physician (many HMO/POS plans do), that naming is a formal step with the plan — complete it, and verify the doctor is in-network for your exact plan at the location you’ll visit.
Lane note — welfare: an assigned doctor is a default, not a destiny — in most programs you may change the assignment within the windows your state and plan publish. Keep the assignment letter — it proves who was assigned, when, and your change rights. An assigned doctor you’ve never seen is this lane’s version of having no doctor.
Lane note — cash: no directory — your list is nearby practices and recommendations; your verification is price as much as participation. Membership / direct-primary-care offers: get the terms in writing (what the fee covers, what it excludes) and judge them by the cash lane’s rules. This kit makes no recommendation.

KEEP THIS PAPER: The listing you relied on — screenshot or printout, dated — plus your log of both calls: date, name, what they confirmed.

One warning travels forward: even after both checks say yes, the desk can still say no on the day — Stage 4’s desk play covers exactly that moment.

Step 2 — The first call: what to ask the office

DO THIS: One call does most of this stage’s work. Make it before you book anything; log it: date, name, answers.

SAY THIS: “Hello, I’m choosing a primary care doctor and I’m calling to confirm a few things before I schedule. One: is Dr. [name] accepting new patients right now? Two: does the practice take [my exact plan name / my Medicaid managed-care plan / — cash lane: — and if so, what are your new-patient and regular visit prices for someone paying cash]? Three: when is the first available appointment for a new patient? Four: which hospital is Dr. [name] affiliated with — where would I be sent if I needed hospital care?”
  • Accepting new patients — the single most common directory lie. A listing is a snapshot; this answer is today.
  • Participation, in the plan’s exact name — “we take [insurer]” is not “we are in-network for [your plan product].” Ask with the full name from your card.
  • First available appointment — a doctor you can’t see for four months is a doctor you don’t have. Note the wait in your log.
  • Hospital affiliation — ask it now, in calm. Your doctor’s hospital is very likely to be your hospital. You’re choosing the building too.

DO THIS: If any answer fails — not accepting, doesn’t take your plan, unreachable wait — move to the next name and make the same call. Don’t book Step 3’s visit until one office passes all four.

KEEP THIS PAPER: Your log of the call — and of any failed calls before it. The failures document the directory problem, dated, in your hand.

Step 3 — Getting on board: the establish-care visit

  • Most practices won’t treat you as their patient — sick visit, referral, after-hours call — until you’ve had a first establish-care (new-patient) visit.
  • Being established before you’re sick is the entire point of doing it now.

DO THIS: Book it once Step 2’s call passes. Bring:

  • Photo ID · your insurance or program card (cash lane: your agreed price confirmation)
  • A list of your current medications — names and doses, from the bottles
  • Names and locations of your previous doctors and hospitals (Step 4 needs the same list — write it once)
  • Your worksheet
SAY THIS (at booking): “I want to schedule a new-patient, establish-care visit with Dr. [name]. I’m bringing my medication list and my previous providers’ information so you can request my records.”

KEEP THIS PAPER: The visit summary, and the names of everyone you dealt with — the first entry in your care file (Step 5).

Lane note — insured: confirm at booking whether the visit bills as preventive or a regular office visit — they price very differently. If your plan required a formal PCP selection, confirm it’s on file with the plan, not just the office.
Lane note — welfare: if you changed an auto-assigned doctor, confirm the change took effect — with the plan and the new office — before booking. Keep the change confirmation with the assignment letter.
Lane note — cash: confirm the visit’s cash price in writing before the appointment: the price, what’s included (labs? tests?), and who quoted it, in your log.

The steps — both doors end here

Step 4 — Moving your records: they are yours

  • THE RULE: under HIPAA’s right of access, your records are yours — you can get a copy, and direct a copy to your new doctor. Not a courtesy. A right.
  • Providers may require the request in writing, on their form or by letter.

DO THIS: The request that works has the same contents every time: who you are, exactly which records, the date range, where to send them, your signature and date.

SAY THIS (letter or form cover note): “[Your name, date of birth, address, phone]. Under my right of access to my own health records, I am requesting a copy of my medical records for [date range, or: my complete record] — including visit notes, test and imaging results, and my medication history — to be sent to [new doctor’s name, practice, address / fax / portal]. [Or: and I will pick up / please send to me directly.] Please let me know the cost before you process this, and if you need anything further from me to complete it. — [Signature, date.]”

DO THIS: Send it in a way that proves the date — portal message with a screenshot, fax with confirmation, mail with proof of mailing — and log it. Ask every prior provider who holds a meaningful piece: previous primary doctors first.

  • The clock: federal rules generally require action within 30 days, with a limited extension the provider must explain in writing. Many states set a faster deadline — verify yours, and write the due date on your log the day you send the request.
  • The cost: the fee for your own records is limited to a reasonable, cost-based fee — some states cap it lower. A quote that sounds like a wall? Ask for it in writing, itemized.
  • Slow-walked? Delay past the window, silence, or unsupported fees is a deviation. Complaints go to the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services, Office for Civil Rights (OCR) — free, in writing or online, running on your paper.
SAY THIS: “My request went in on [date]. I know there’s a deadline for acting on it, and I’d like to complete this without a complaint. Can you tell me today when my records will be sent?”

KEEP THIS PAPER: A copy of every request, proof of its date, the due date you calculated, and every reply — filed in your care file (Step 5).

Step 5 — Your care file starts here

DO THIS: Start your care file today — one place, paper or digital, holding the human map of your care. Start it with three things:

  • Your My Care Team worksheet (below)
  • The verification notes and establish-care summary from Steps 1–3
  • The records bundle from Step 4

DO THIS: Fill in the worksheet in one sitting, from this stage’s papers. A blank line is a to-do, not a failure — close the blanks within the week. Everything after this stage gets filed behind it, in order.

Every year, run it again

DO THIS: Every new plan year, run the two confirmations again — even if nothing in your life changed. Plans rebuild networks; doctors change contracts; nobody mails you a notice. One short check a year is the cheapest step in this kit.

KEEP THIS PAPER: The dated log of each year’s check, next to your plan card.

The mistakes — yours to not make

  1. Picking a doctor from a list and never verifying — then meeting the truth during an illness.
  2. Verifying with only one party. One confirmation is half a fact.
  3. Never asking the hospital affiliation — and discovering which building your doctor chose for you, at the worst moment.
  4. Drifting without a primary doctor because the assigned one looked official. An unchosen doctor is the same as none.
  5. Booking sick care where you were never established — and getting the stranger’s version of the first visit.
  6. Assuming your records followed you. They move when you move them, in writing, on a clock you track.
  7. Asking for records verbally, with no dated copy of the request.
  8. Starting no care file — so Stage 6’s fight runs on memory.

The deviations — theirs to answer for

  1. Listings naming doctors who aren’t accepting patients, aren’t participating, or aren’t at the listed practice.
  2. An office confirming an insurer generally while not in-network for your specific plan product.
  3. An auto-assignment treated as your informed choice, with your change rights nowhere in the letter.
  4. Records requests ignored past the federal window (or your state’s faster one), with no written explanation.
  5. Record fees quoted as flat, unexplained sums instead of the reasonable, cost-based fee.
  6. An establish-care visit billed under a different visit type than the one described at booking.

What “done” looks like for Stage 3:

  • You can name your doctor, their practice, and their hospital — and it’s written on your worksheet.
  • Verified twice, with dated notes. Established, or the visit is booked.
  • Records requested, due dates on your log. Care file started.

My Care Team — the worksheet

Your facts stay yours. Nothing here is uploaded or sent anywhere — it exists in exactly one place: your hands. Fill it in one sitting; update it the day anything changes. Keep it at the front of your care file.

My lane + plan / program — insured, cash-pay, or welfare, with the exact name.

Member / ID number — copied from the card. Offices ask for it on every call.

Primary doctor — the verified one, full name as the practice uses it.

Practice name + address — the office you’ll actually visit.

Practice phone — including any separate scheduling or nurse line.

Patient portal — the portal name or web address, and where your login is kept (not the password). Portal messages are dated writing — this kit values them.

Established? (date) — the date of your establish-care visit, or the date it’s booked for.

Verified with plan / agency (date) — when they confirmed this doctor for you, and who confirmed it.

Hospital affiliation — your doctor’s hospital. Probably your hospital, if it ever matters.

Specialists I use — name, specialty, practice phone, one line each.

Pharmacy — name, location, phone.

Records: where my history lives — previous doctors, hospitals, clinics, one line each, with “requested (date) / received (date)” beside each.

Records now held by my primary doctor — what has arrived, checked off as it lands.

Print this and keep it at the front of your care file.
