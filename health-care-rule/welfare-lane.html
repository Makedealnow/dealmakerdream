Medicaid or state coverage — your job is keeping the card.

  • Four questions tell you you’re in the right lane:

1. Does your card say Medicaid — or a state program name, or a health plan’s name with Medicaid on it?

2. Did a state or county office decide you qualify — and does keeping the card depend on answering their letters and renewing on time?

3. Do you pay little or nothing at the doctor’s office — because the program pays, not you?

4. Do official letters scare you a little — deadlines, notices, words like termination and appeal?

If you said yes — especially to the first two — this is your lane. In this lane, the coverage itself is the asset: your job is keeping the card, and the program’s payment is the whole payment.

Step 1 — Know exactly what you have

DO THIS: Before anything else, answer three questions and write the answers down: Which program? (Medicaid itself goes by a state name in most states — Healthy Connections, SoonerCare, Apple Health, and dozens more; the name on your card or your approval letter is the one that counts.) Which plan? (Most Medicaid members are in a managed-care plan — a company name on the card alongside the program’s.) When does it renew? (Coverage runs in periods — usually twelve months — and the renewal month is the most important date in this lane.)

SAY THIS (if you’re not sure what you have): “I need to confirm what coverage I have. Can you tell me the program name, the plan name if I’m in a managed-care plan, and the month my coverage renews? I’m writing it down.”

KEEP THIS PAPER: The approval letter, the card (photograph both sides), and every renewal notice. Photograph them into your phone album the day they arrive.

Step 2 — Renewals: the step this lane lives or dies on

DO THIS: When the renewal packet comes, treat it like a bill with a deadline — because it is one, and the price of missing it is the coverage itself. Open it the day it arrives. Note the deadline. Return what’s asked, the way they ask for it, before the date. Keep proof: a photograph of the completed form, the mailing receipt or the portal confirmation.

SAY THIS (if anything in the packet is unclear): “I’ve received my renewal packet and I want to get it right. Can you tell me exactly what documents you need from me, and the last day they can arrive? — and your name, please, for my records.”

KEEP THIS PAPER: A copy of everything you send, with the date you sent it. In this lane, sent and provable beats sent.

If this happens

You missed the renewal deadline

Act the same week — there is usually a reconsideration window (commonly 90 days after the termination) during which returning the renewal restores coverage, often back to the termination date. Call the agency, say the words reconsideration period, and ask what to submit. After that window, it’s a new application — slower, and with a gap. Either way: the worst move is silence. Call.

Your address and your circumstances are part of the coverage

DO THIS: Report changes the way your state requires — a move, a change in household, a change in income — within the window your approval letter states (commonly 10 days). And keep your mailing address current above all: in this lane, the mail is the battlefield. Most coverage losses aren’t decisions about eligibility — they’re letters that went to an old address and deadlines that passed unanswered.

KEEP THIS PAPER: Every change you report, with the date and how you reported it.

Step 3 — Every official letter: the protocol

Open it the day it arrives. Photograph it. Find the deadline and write it on the letter itself. Then act inside the window — answer, appeal, or call, in that order of preference. A notice you disagree with is not a final word; it’s the start of a process with rules, and the next step of this stage is that process.

If this happens

A notice says your coverage ends, or a service is denied

A decision against you must come as advance written notice — generally at least 10 days before the effective date — stating the action, the reasons, and your appeal rights. Appeal before the effective date, or within the window the notice states (often 10 days from the notice date), and your benefits generally continue while the appeal is decided — that timing is the whole game, so the notice’s own dates govern: read them off the letter, not from memory. In a managed-care plan, the appeal runs to the plan first, then to a state fair hearing if the plan rules against you. A denial of a service your doctor requested follows the same road: plan appeal, then fair hearing.

Step 4 — Finding a doctor: the plan works for you here

DO THIS: Use the plan’s directory, then verify — office and plan, both, logged with names and dates, exactly like the insured lane’s Stage 3. The difference in your lane: if no in-network doctor is available within the plan’s access standards, the plan must arrange care — out of network if necessary, at no extra cost to you. A directory full of dead ends isn’t your failure; it’s the plan’s problem to solve.

SAY THIS: “I’ve called [number] doctors in your directory and none is accepting patients / the wait is [length]. I need [the care]. Please arrange an appointment within your access standards, or authorize one out of network at no extra cost to me. Who is handling this, and when will I hear back?”

KEEP THIS PAPER: Your call log — every office called, every answer. In this lane, the log is leverage: it’s the record that turns “I can’t find a doctor” into the plan’s documented obligation.

Katie’s rule for this stage

THE RULE: for a service your program covers, a provider who takes your coverage accepts the program’s payment as payment in full — beyond any small copay your state allows, the bill is not yours.

THE CHECK: every bill, compared against one question: was this a covered service, from a provider in my program, while my coverage was active?

THE MEANING: if the answer is yes and a bill says otherwise, the bill is the deviation — not your debt.

Step 5 — At the desk: what’s different in your lane

DO THIS: Present the card, and confirm the office takes your plan — the specific managed-care plan, not just “Medicaid” — for this year. If the office says it doesn’t take your plan, don’t decide at the desk: step aside and call the plan (the number on the card). The plan either confirms, finds you an office that does, or starts the arrangement it owes you.

SAY THIS (if a bill for a covered service reaches you): “This service was covered by [program/plan] on [date], and I was an active member. My understanding is that the program’s payment is payment in full for covered services, apart from any copay in my plan. Please correct the billing and bill the plan. I’ll note today’s date and your name.”

KEEP THIS PAPER: The card, the eligibility confirmations, and any bill that arrives — a wrong bill is evidence, not trash.

If this happens — the clipboard trap

A form asks you to agree to pay

Read anything that asks for your signature on money twice. For a service your program covers, the program’s payment is payment in full by law — a form you sign cannot lawfully move that bill onto you, whatever its wording says. A payment agreement can bind you only for a service the program genuinely doesn’t cover, and then only when you were told so, specifically and in writing, before the service. If you’re handed such a form and you’re not certain the service is non-covered, don’t sign at the desk: photograph it, call the plan, and let the plan say whether the service is covered before your signature says anything.

Step 6 — Medicare too? The QMB shield

If you have both Medicare and Medicaid and your Medicaid includes the Qualified Medicare Beneficiary (QMB) program, federal law is at its most explicit: providers may not bill you for Medicare’s deductibles, coinsurance, or copays — at all, in any amount, whether or not your state’s Medicaid pays the provider anything toward them. A QMB bill is never a bill you negotiate; it’s a bill you return for correction.

SAY THIS: “I’m a Qualified Medicare Beneficiary. Federal law prohibits billing me for Medicare cost-sharing. Please remove this charge and bill the state. If it isn’t corrected, I’ll be reporting it to 1-800-MEDICARE.”

KEEP THIS PAPER: The bill itself — if it has to be reported, the bill is the documentation.

Step 7 — When you disagree: the ladder, in order

1. The plan’s appeal (managed care) — call the number on the card or the notice; note the deadline on the notice and appeal inside it. Appeal before the effective date to keep benefits running.

2. The state fair hearing — if the plan rules against you, you can take the decision to a state hearing officer. The request window is stated on the plan’s decision letter — commonly up to 90 days from the notice; the letter’s number governs.

3. The agency’s complaint routes — your state Medicaid agency, its managed-care ombudsman if it has one, and your state’s protection-and-advocacy organization for disability-related disputes.

SAY THIS (asking for the hearing): “I’m requesting a state fair hearing on the decision in this notice dated [date]. I’m within the window stated on the letter. Please confirm my request is recorded today, and send me the hearing information in writing.”

KEEP THIS PAPER: Every notice, every appeal, every decision — in order, in one folder. A hearing is won on the paper trail as much as on the facts.

The mistakes — yours to not make

  1. Letting mail sit. In this lane, an unopened envelope can end coverage. Open everything the day it comes.
  2. Missing the renewal — or returning it without keeping proof you did.
  3. Moving without updating the address. The notices go to the address on file, and the deadlines run whether you saw them or not.
  4. Paying a bill for a covered service because it arrived with your name on it.
  5. Signing a payment agreement at the desk for a service you hadn’t confirmed was non-covered.
  6. Appealing late. The continuation of benefits lives inside the window on the notice; outside it, you’re asking instead of invoking.
  7. Accepting “no doctors are available” as the end — in this lane it’s the plan’s assignment, not yours.

The deviations — theirs to answer for

  1. A bill sent to you for a covered service beyond any allowed copay.
  2. A QMB patient billed for Medicare cost-sharing — prohibited outright.
  3. Coverage ended without advance written notice, or a notice missing the reasons or the appeal rights.
  4. A clipboard form used to shift a covered bill onto the patient.
  5. A plan directory full of doctors who aren’t taking patients, with no arrangement made.
  6. An appeal acknowledged late, decided late, or never answered.

What “done” looks like for this stage:

  • You can say, from your own notes: your program, your plan, your renewal month.
  • Every notice in your folder has its deadline written on it.
  • You know the sentence that ends a wrong bill: the program’s payment is payment in full.

From here, your road joins everyone else’s. The shared stages ahead start with your doctor (Stage 3) — that’s your next step below — then the visit itself (Stage 4), what happens after (Stage 5), and the bill (Stage 6) — written for all three lanes, with welfare-lane notes wherever your rules differ.

Read this notice for me

Question 1 of 2

You’ll need: The notice itself — the whole letter, every page, photographed flat and readable.

This question reads the letter the way this stage taught you to: what it decides, what it’s asking, and the date that matters.

This whole box is what gets copied — word for word, including the last paragraph.

I receive health coverage through a public program, and an agency or plan has sent me a notice. Below I will paste the full text of the notice. Read only the notice and give me back: 1. Who sent it — the agency or the plan — using the name printed on the notice. 2. What the notice says is happening or changing, in plain language: a renewal, a change, a denial, a termination — and what exactly it applies to. 3. Every date the notice states, copied exactly: the date of the notice, any effective date for the change, and every deadline — to respond, to return a form, or to appeal. List each date with what the notice says it is the deadline FOR. 4. What the notice says I must do, and by when — in the order the notice gives. 5. What the notice says about my right to appeal or request a hearing, quoting its words — and whether it says anything about my benefits continuing while an appeal is decided. 6. Anything the notice does NOT state: no reason given, no deadline given, no appeal rights mentioned. Label each "Not in the notice." Work only from what I pasted. If something is not in the pasted material, write "Not in the document" — do not guess, and do not use outside knowledge to fill the gap. Do not invent numbers, dates, deadlines, or rules. Use plain language.

If your situation involves a lawsuit, a collections action, or a deadline in court papers, talk to a lawyer.

One more thing, specific to this lane: Medicaid is run by each state under federal rules, so program names, renewal procedures, copay amounts, appeal addresses, and managed-care arrangements differ from state to state — sometimes a great deal. The federal floor described in this stage — advance notice, appeal and fair-hearing rights, payment-in-full for covered services, the QMB billing prohibition — is the part that holds everywhere. The details that fill it in live in your state’s notices and your plan’s member handbook, and those documents govern whenever they differ from the general descriptions here.
