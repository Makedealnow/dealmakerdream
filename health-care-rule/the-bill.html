The bill has arrived — audit it before you pay it. Most of the time this is a calm, line-by-line check against paper you already hold. This kit is a watch, not a job: if everything went smooth, there is little to do. This stage is here for when it didn’t.

Every stage before this one was moving toward this page. Stage 1 taught you your plan’s numbers. Stage 3 verified the doctors. Stage 4 disciplined your signature and started your log. Stage 5 named the cast before the day. Now the bills arrive — and all of that paper stops being preparation and becomes position. The billing office works from its file: codes, dates, balances. You work from your file: the estimates, the authorizations, the cast list, the logs with dates and names. When the two files disagree, the one that is documented, dated, and written down wins arguments that memory never does.

One honesty note: most first bills are not fraud. They are the output of a sloppy, hurried, code-driven system that bills what it can and corrects what it’s made to correct. That is not a reason to pay them. It is the reason this stage exists.

Katie’s rule for this stage

THE RULE: you are owed an itemized bill — every charge on its own line — and billing has to match the rules and your paper: your plan’s allowed amounts and cost-sharing, your estimate or agreed price, your coverage, and the protections the earlier stages set up.

THE CHECK: the line-by-line audit against your own records. Assemble your paper — plan card, desk quotes, lobby-call reference numbers, referral logs, authorizations — and match every line to care you actually received, from a biller you expected, at a status you verified.

THE MEANING: every mismatch is a named deviation with a remedy, presented as an audit, not a plea. You are not begging a billing office for mercy. You are showing it, line by line, where its bill breaks its own rules — and asking it to correct the bill.

Step 1 — First rule: never pay the first bill

EOB first — the discipline that comes before everything

DO THIS: A first bill is a claim, not a verdict. It is the provider’s opening statement about what you owe — frequently produced before your plan has finished processing. Before you pay anything, do three things, in this order: get the itemized bill, assemble your paper, and match them. None of the three is optional.

Lane note — insured (EOB first): you need two documents before the matching starts, not one: the itemized bill from the provider and the Explanation of Benefits (EOB) from your plan for the same dates. The EOB arrives first in the normal order of things — and it says, in so many words, “this is not a bill.” Believe it: you never pay an EOB. You pay a provider bill that matches one. If the EOB hasn’t arrived, the claim may not have finished processing — and a provider bill sent before the plan has paid its share is one of the most common first-bill errors in existence. Wait for the pair.
Lane note — cash: your second document is the good-faith estimate plus any written agreed price. If you never received an estimate for scheduled care, that absence goes in your log too — it is itself material at Step 3.
Lane note — welfare: your second document is your coverage itself — the notice or card showing you were covered on the date of service. For a covered service from a participating provider, Step 2 of this stage has a short answer, and it is not a number.

The itemized-bill demand

DO THIS: A summary bill — one line, one total, one due date — cannot be audited. It is not really a bill; it is a number with a deadline attached. You are entitled to an itemized statement: every charge on its own line, with the date of service, a description, the quantity, and the price. The day a bill arrives, before paying a cent: request the itemized version, in writing or by a logged call, and note the request in your log with the date. Do not let a due date printed on a summary bill rush you.

SAY THIS: “Before I pay anything on this account, I need the fully itemized bill — every charge on its own line, with dates of service, descriptions, quantities, and prices, for [date(s) of service]. A summary total isn’t something I can verify. Please send the itemization, and note on the account that I’ve requested it and am reviewing the charges.”

KEEP THIS PAPER: The itemized bill when it arrives, the summary bill it replaces, and your log entry of the request — date, name, and what you were told about when it will come.

Step 2 — The audit: assemble your paper, match line by line

Assemble the kit’s paper

DO THIS: Pull the episode’s file — everything the earlier stages told you to keep, now in one stack, in this order:

  • Your plan card — your plan’s money words (Stage 1).
  • The EOBs for the episode (insured) — the plan’s own account of what it allowed, paid, and assigned to you.
  • The good-faith estimate and any written agreed price (cash).
  • Desk quotes — what you were quoted at the desk, and by whom (Stage 4).
  • Lobby-call reference numbers — the reference numbers and names from any lobby call to your plan (Stage 4’s desk play).
  • Referral logs — who referred what, to whom, and when.
  • The cast list (Stage 5) — every biller you expected, with the verification or price you recorded beside each name.
  • The authorizations — numbers, scopes, dates.
  • Your visit log and episode log — who you saw, what was done, what was promised, and when.
  • Any notices and forms from the episode — including, especially, anything you signed, and anything you declined to sign.

If pieces are missing, note the gap rather than abandoning the audit. A partial file still beats the billing office’s memory, and several of the checks below run on the bill alone.

Match — the three piles

DO THIS: Work the itemized bill against your file, line by line, with a pencil. Every line should trace to three things — care you actually received, from a biller you expected, at a status you verified. A line that fails any of the three gets circled, not paid. When the audit is done, the bill is sorted into three piles:

  1. Checks out — lines that match your paper. A bill that passes, line by line, is a bill you actually owe — Step 4 covers paying, negotiating, or planning it.
  2. Errors & deviations — lines that fail a check. Each circle becomes a numbered item in Step 3’s letter.
  3. Awaiting explanation — lines neither the bill nor your paper explains. You ask about those in writing, before paying.

Identity checks — is this your bill at all? Wrong person, wrong dates, wrong episode. Duplicate charges — the same service, same date, charged twice; the single most common itemized-bill error. Services and supplies never received — make that claim from your log, not from a feeling, and write the log reference beside the circle.

Quantity and arithmetic checks. Wrong quantities and durations (ten units billed, six given; three days billed, two stayed). Wrong dates. Plain arithmetic — add the lines; a total that exceeds its own lines is a circle by itself.

Status checks. Was this billed at the status you verified? Your share on the provider bill should equal what the EOB assigned to you — not the provider’s sticker price minus what the plan happened to pay.

SAY THIS (for a line you cannot identify): “Line [number] on the itemized bill is described as [description], for [amount]. I don’t recognize this charge. Please explain in writing what service it represents, when it was provided, and where its price was disclosed to me before the service.” A charge they cannot explain is a charge that frequently does not survive being explained.

KEEP THIS PAPER: The itemized bill with your circles and notes, and the Bill Audit Worksheet (at the bottom of this page) filled in as you worked. The letter in Step 3 is written from the worksheet; the worksheet is the letter’s evidence index.

If this happens

The ancillary cast bills out-of-network at an in-network facility

Lane note — insured: an out-of-network balance bill from anesthesiology, radiology, pathology, lab, or an assistant surgeon for care at an in-network facility is a No Surprises deviation — the exact case the federal law was written to eliminate. Circle it, note “Stage 5, Step 3” beside it, and it goes in the letter by name. The same protection, emergency chapter: your share for emergency services is the in-network cost-sharing, wherever the emergency was treated. Anything above it is a deviation, not a balance.
If this happens

A bill arrives for a covered service in the welfare lane

Lane note — welfare: a participating provider accepts the program’s payment as payment in full for covered services, apart from any small allowed copay your state sets. A balance bill in this lane is not a negotiation opener; it is a deviation to be corrected in writing, and if it persists, routed to the state agency. QMB readers: a bill for Medicare deductibles, coinsurance, or copays is the same deviation in its most clearly forbidden form — do not pay it; the route is the billing office first, then 1-800-MEDICARE and your state Medicaid agency, with the bills kept as the documentation they are.
If this happens

The final bill outruns the good-faith estimate (cash lane)

Lane note — cash: compare per provider, the way the estimate was given: a final bill from a provider $400 or more above that provider’s good-faith estimate opens the federal patient-provider dispute resolution process — a formal dispute you must start within 120 calendar days of the date on the initial bill — write that deadline down the week a too-high bill arrives. Below that threshold, the difference is still a Step 3 letter item: an estimate is called good-faith for a reason, and a provider should have to explain a bill that outran it.

Also watch for: bills from billers never on the cast list and never disclosed — a name on neither the cast list nor your episode log is a question with paperwork attached: who are you, who ordered this, and where was it disclosed? And facility fees and similar lines whose legitimacy depends on the setting: this kit will not tell you any category is always improper, because that isn’t true — a line you cannot identify is a line you ask about, in writing, before paying.

Step 3 — The deviation letter: put it in writing, put a hold on the account

Phone calls open this fight; paper moves it. A billing office can out-wait a caller, transfer a caller, and forget a caller. It cannot as easily ignore a dated letter, sent with proof of delivery, that lists specific violations and demands a specific status on the account — because that letter creates the file that every later rung of the ladder (Step 5) will read.

DO THIS — the call that precedes it: One call first — not to argue the bill line by line, but to establish the account’s status while the letter travels:

SAY THIS: “I’m calling about account [number], for service on [date]. I’ve received the itemized bill and I’m disputing specific charges in writing — the letter is going out [today / this week] with delivery confirmation. I’m asking you to place the account in dispute status with a billing hold now, so nothing is sent to collections while the dispute is open, and to note that I called today. Please give me your name, and confirm the hold and the note.”

Log the name, the date, and the confirmation — and then send the letter even if the call went beautifully. Verbal holds evaporate; the letter is the hold’s evidence.

Step 4 — Negotiate what honestly remains

Errors struck, explanations received, protections applied — what remains is the corrected balance: the amount that survives your audit as genuinely yours. Pay the undisputed part — nobody negotiates before the audit, because negotiating an unaudited bill means bargaining over their errors as if they were your debts. After the audit, the negotiation is honest, and there are three moves, in order.

MOVE 1 — ASK FOR THE PROMPT-PAY / CASH DISCOUNT on the corrected balance: a balance paid in full, now, is worth a discount to almost every provider — and the discount is asked for, not offered.

SAY THIS: “The corrected balance is [amount], after the disputed charges were removed. If I pay it in full this week, what discount can you offer for prompt payment? — and if we agree on a figure, I’ll need it in writing, showing the account settled in full at that amount, before I pay.”

MOVE 2 — APPLY FOR FINANCIAL ASSISTANCE, EVEN MID-FIGHT: if the corrected balance is still beyond you, apply under the hospital’s financial assistance policy now — nonprofit hospitals are required to have such a policy, publish it, and accept applications, and applications can be made at essentially any point in the billing cycle, including while a dispute is open. A complete application generally requires the hospital to hold off on extraordinary collection actions while it is being decided — one more reason to file it completely and keep the proof of filing.

SAY THIS: “The corrected balance is still more than I can pay. I want to apply under the hospital’s financial assistance policy. Please send me the policy and the application, tell me exactly what documentation you need, and note on the account that an application is being filed. I’ll return it complete by [date], and I want confirmation when it’s received.”

MOVE 3 — IF YOU NEED A PAYMENT PLAN, GET IT IN WRITING BEFORE THE FIRST PAYMENT: the total being paid (the corrected balance, at any agreed discount), the installment amount and dates, whether interest or fees apply, what happens if a payment is late, and confirmation that the account stays out of collections while the plan is current. Get every agreement in writing before paying.

KEEP THIS PAPER: The assistance policy and application with proof of filing and the decision; the discount agreement; the payment-plan terms and every payment confirmation. A “settled” account with no paper is an account that can be re-billed.

Lane note — welfare: this step mostly does not apply to you, and that is the shield working: a covered service’s remainder is the program’s, not yours. If what remains is a bill your lane says should never have existed, skip this step entirely and go to Step 5: you don’t negotiate a deviation, you get it corrected.

Step 5 — The escalation ladder, assembled

Every earlier stage built a piece of this ladder. Here it is, assembled, in the kit’s fixed order — the system’s own processes first, the regulators matched to the violation next, the attorney last.

RUNG 1 — THE PROVIDER’S BILLING OFFICE: The deviation letter (Step 3) and the negotiation (Step 4) live here. Most billing fights end on this rung, because most bills cannot survive their own itemization once someone reads it with a file in hand.

RUNG 2 — THE PROVIDER’S COMPLIANCE OFFICE / PATIENT ADVOCATE: the office whose job is the institution’s conduct, not its collections.

SAY THIS: “This dispute has been open since [date of letter] without a substantive answer. Please transfer the file to your compliance office — or connect me with the patient advocate — and give me that office’s direct contact and a reference number for the escalation.”

RUNG 3 — THE PLAN-SIDE LADDER: when the fight is about the plan’s decision — a denied claim, a refused authorization, a miscalculated cost-share — the fight belongs on the ladder Stage 2 built: plan appeal (internal appeal), then external review, with the billing dispute and the claim appeal run as the separate tracks they are. (Welfare lane: plan appeal first, then the state fair hearing.)

RUNG 4 — THE REGULATORS, MATCHED TO THE VIOLATION: file at the door that matches the deviation — and file with the packet: the letter, the log, the documents, in order.

  • Plan conduct — denials handled in bad faith, blown appeal deadlines, ghost-network listings, a plan that won’t honor its own contract → your state department of insurance (state insurance department). Forks: self-funded employer plans route to the U.S. Department of Labor; Medicare Advantage complaints run through Medicare itself.
  • Surprise-bill violations — balance bills the No Surprises Act forbids → the federal No Surprises help desk, run through the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS, cms.gov, under “No Surprises”).
  • Provider billing practices generally — patterns of improper billing, charges for care never rendered, a billing office that will not correct a documented error → your state attorney general’s consumer protection office.
  • Hospital conduct — screening or discharge-rule violations, price-transparency failures → CMS and your state hospital-licensing agency.
  • Welfare-lane violations — improper balance billing, notice and hearing failures → your state Medicaid agency and its managed-care ombudsman first, with HHS as the federal backstop.

RUNG 5 — THE ATTORNEY, LAST: you don’t need an attorney until you’ve run through the regulations that exist for you to use. And if the case ever does reach this rung, you arrive as the client attorneys hope for: a clean record, rights preserved from the beginning, every deviation documented with dates and delivery proofs, every rung below climbed and logged. (Welfare lane: remember your attorney rung may be free — legal aid takes these cases at no charge in every state.)

One calibration: no rung guarantees an outcome, and each office decides on its own timeline. What the ladder guarantees is sequence and weight — every rung adds a file, a deadline, and a more serious reader.

Step 6 — Collections defense

If this happens

A bill reaches a collector anyway

DO NOT PANIC-PAY: A collector’s first letter is built to produce exactly one thing: a fast payment from a frightened person, ideally of an amount nobody has verified. It is Step 1’s first rule in a new envelope: a collector’s claim is a claim, not a verdict — and federal law gives you a specific tool for it.

The validation request. Under federal debt-collection law (the Fair Debt Collection Practices Act), a third-party collector must send you information about the debt, and if you dispute the debt in writing within the window stated in their notice — commonly 30 days from their first written notice — they must stop collecting until they verify the debt and mail you that verification. Verification should connect the collector to the original account: the provider, the dates, the amount, and their right to collect it.

DO THIS: The week a collector’s first letter arrives: answer it in writing, inside the window their notice states, disputing the debt and requesting validation — and if the underlying bill is already disputed with the provider, say so, with the letter’s date. Send it with delivery proof, keep copies of everything, and log it. Then continue the underlying fight on the ladder (Step 5) from the paper — a collector’s pause is breathing room, not a resolution.

SAY THIS (the validation request — adapt the brackets): “[Your name and address]. [Date]. Re: your letter dated [date], account/reference [number], alleged balance [amount], original provider [name]. I dispute this debt [in full / in the amount of (disputed portion)] and request validation, including an itemized statement of the charges, the dates of service, and documentation of your authority to collect this account. The underlying charges were disputed in writing with the provider on [date of your deviation letter] and that dispute [is open / resulted in (outcome)]. Please do not contact me about this debt again until you have mailed the requested validation. All further communication with me about this debt should be in writing. — [Your name.]”

If the “collector” is the provider itself, the federal collection statute’s main rules generally govern third-party collectors rather than a provider collecting its own bills — but state consumer-protection law, the provider’s own dispute obligations, and every rung of Step 5 still apply, and the written-dispute discipline is identical.

Medical debt and your credit report — stated honestly, because it changes. As of this kit’s writing, the three credit bureaus’ voluntary practices remove paid medical collections from credit reports and exclude medical collections under $500, and medical collections generally do not appear until they are at least a year old. Those are industry policies, not permanent law — they can change, and a federal rule that would have gone further was struck down in court in 2025, so do not build a plan on today’s version lasting. Several states separately restrict or prohibit medical-debt credit reporting. Verify the current state of play — your state attorney general’s consumer page and the bureaus’ own announcements are the sources — before deciding how much a reporting threat weighs.

And the standing rule for this whole step: keep disputing from the paper, not from memory. The collector has a balance and a script. You have the itemization with the circles, the letter with the delivery receipt, the log with the names.

The mistakes — yours to not make

  1. Paying the first bill — any first bill — before the itemization, the EOB or estimate, and the match.
  2. Trying to audit a summary bill: one line, one total, nothing to check.
  3. Paying an EOB. It says it isn’t a bill; believe it.
  4. Disputing by phone only — no letter, no delivery proof, no hold confirmed — and discovering the “dispute” existed nowhere but the call.
  5. Negotiating a discount on an unaudited bill, bargaining over their errors as if they were your debts.
  6. Skipping the financial assistance application because the bill is “already in dispute” — the application runs on its own track, at any point in the cycle.
  7. Making the first payment-plan installment before the plan’s terms are in your hand in writing.
  8. Ignoring a collector’s letter until its dispute window closes — the validation right has a clock, and the clock is in their notice.
  9. Panic-paying a collector to make the letters stop, on an amount nobody verified.
  10. Arguing any of it from memory — reconstructing dates, quotes, and promises instead of reading them off the log you were supposed to keep. (If your log is thin, start it today, from the paper you do have; a late log beats a perfect memory.)

The deviations — theirs to answer for

  1. A bill sent before the claim finished processing — the provider’s total presented as your balance while the plan’s share was still in transit.
  2. Duplicate charges, charges for care never received, quantities and dates the record doesn’t support — billed first, corrected only when caught.
  3. A balance bill from an out-of-network ancillary provider for care at an in-network facility, or above in-network cost-sharing for emergency care — the No Surprises core cases, arriving anyway.
  4. A bill to a Medicaid patient for a covered service beyond allowed cost-sharing — and its QMB form, Medicare cost-sharing billed to the one patient federal law says may never be billed it.
  5. A cash patient’s final bill $400 or more above the good-faith estimate, with the dispute right nowhere mentioned on the statement.
  6. A disputed account sent to collections while a written dispute sat unanswered — the hold requested, the letter delivered, the escalation made anyway.
  7. A financial assistance policy that exists on paper and is never mentioned by the billing office across months of collection calls.
  8. A collector pursuing a balance it cannot validate — no itemization, no authority, nothing but a number bought in a spreadsheet.
