The bill has arrived — audit it before you pay it.

  • Most of the time: a calm, line-by-line check against paper you already hold.
  • This kit is a watch, not a job — if everything went smooth, there’s little to do. This stage is here for when it didn’t.
  • Most first bills aren’t fraud — they’re a sloppy, code-driven system that bills what it can and corrects what it’s made to correct. That’s not a reason to pay them. It’s the reason this stage exists.

Katie’s rule for this stage

THE RULE: you are owed an itemized bill — every charge on its own line — and billing has to match the rules and your paper: your plan’s allowed amounts, your estimate or agreed price, your coverage, and the protections the earlier stages set up.

THE CHECK: the line-by-line audit against your own records — plan card, desk quotes, lobby-call reference numbers, referral logs, authorizations. Every line traces to care you actually received, from a biller you expected, at a status you verified.

THE MEANING: every mismatch is a named deviation with a remedy — presented as an audit, not a plea. You’re showing the billing office where its bill breaks its own rules, and asking it to correct the bill.

Step 1 — First rule: never pay the first bill

EOB first — the discipline that comes before everything

DO THIS: A first bill is a claim, not a verdict — frequently produced before your plan has finished processing. Before you pay anything, three things, in order: get the itemized bill, assemble your paper, and match them. None of the three is optional.

Lane note — insured (EOB first): you need two documents: the itemized bill and the Explanation of Benefits (EOB) from your plan for the same dates. The EOB says, in so many words, “this is not a bill” — believe it: you never pay an EOB. You pay a provider bill that matches one. No EOB yet? The claim may not have finished processing — wait for the pair.
Lane note — cash: your second document is the good-faith estimate plus any written agreed price. Never received an estimate for scheduled care? That absence goes in your log too — it’s material at Step 3.
Lane note — welfare: your second document is your coverage itself — the notice or card showing you were covered on the date of service. For a covered service from a participating provider, Step 2 has a short answer, and it is not a number.

The itemized-bill demand

DO THIS: A summary bill — one line, one total, one due date — cannot be audited. You are entitled to an itemized statement: every charge on its own line, with the date of service, a description, the quantity, and the price. The day a bill arrives, before paying a cent: request the itemized version, in writing or by a logged call, and note the request in your log. Don’t let a due date on a summary bill rush you.

SAY THIS: “Before I pay anything on this account, I need the fully itemized bill — every charge on its own line, with dates of service, descriptions, quantities, and prices, for [date(s) of service]. A summary total isn’t something I can verify. Please send the itemization, and note on the account that I’ve requested it and am reviewing the charges.”

KEEP THIS PAPER: The itemized bill, the summary bill it replaces, and your log entry of the request — date, name, and when it will come.

Step 2 — The audit: assemble your paper, match line by line

Assemble the kit’s paper

DO THIS: Pull the episode’s file — everything the earlier stages told you to keep, in one stack, in this order:

  • Your plan card (Stage 1).
  • The EOBs for the episode (insured).
  • The good-faith estimate and any written agreed price (cash).
  • Desk quotes (Stage 4) · Lobby-call reference numbers (Stage 4).
  • Referral logs · The cast list (Stage 5) · The authorizations.
  • Your visit log and episode log.
  • Any notices and forms from the episode — especially anything you signed, and anything you declined to sign.

Pieces missing? Note the gap rather than abandoning the audit — several checks below run on the bill alone.

Match — the three piles

DO THIS: Work the itemized bill against your file, line by line, with a pencil. Every line should trace to three things — care you actually received, from a biller you expected, at a status you verified. A line that fails any of the three gets circled, not paid. The bill sorts into three piles:

  1. Checks out — lines that match your paper. A bill that passes is a bill you actually owe — Step 4 covers paying it.
  2. Errors & deviations — lines that fail a check. Each circle becomes a numbered item in Step 3’s letter.
  3. Awaiting explanation — lines neither the bill nor your paper explains. You ask about those in writing, before paying.
  • Identity checks: wrong person, wrong dates, wrong episode — is this your bill at all?
  • Duplicate charges — the same service, same date, charged twice. The single most common itemized-bill error.
  • Services never received — claim it from your log, not a feeling; write the log reference beside the circle.
  • Quantity and arithmetic: ten units billed, six given; three days billed, two stayed. Add the lines — a total that exceeds its own lines is a circle by itself.
  • Status checks: your share on the provider bill should equal what the EOB assigned to you — not the sticker price minus what the plan happened to pay.
SAY THIS (for a line you cannot identify): “Line [number] on the itemized bill is described as [description], for [amount]. I don’t recognize this charge. Please explain in writing what service it represents, when it was provided, and where its price was disclosed to me before the service.”

KEEP THIS PAPER: The itemized bill with your circles and notes, and the Bill Audit Worksheet (bottom of this page) filled in as you worked. The letter in Step 3 is written from the worksheet.

If this happens

The ancillary cast bills out-of-network at an in-network facility

Lane note — insured: an out-of-network balance bill from anesthesiology, radiology, pathology, lab, or an assistant surgeon for care at an in-network facility is a No Surprises deviation — the exact case the federal law was written to eliminate. Circle it, note “Stage 5, Step 3” beside it, and it goes in the letter by name. Same protection, emergency chapter: your share for emergency services is the in-network cost-sharing, wherever the emergency was treated. Anything above it is a deviation, not a balance.
If this happens

A bill arrives for a covered service in the welfare lane

Lane note — welfare: a participating provider accepts the program’s payment as payment in full for covered services, apart from any small allowed copay your state sets. A balance bill in this lane is not a negotiation opener; it is a deviation to be corrected in writing, and if it persists, routed to the state agency. QMB readers: a bill for Medicare deductibles, coinsurance, or copays is the same deviation in its most clearly forbidden form — do not pay it; the route is the billing office first, then 1-800-MEDICARE and your state Medicaid agency, with the bills kept as the documentation they are.
If this happens

The final bill outruns the good-faith estimate (cash lane)

Lane note — cash: compare per provider, the way the estimate was given: a final bill from a provider $400 or more above that provider’s good-faith estimate opens the federal patient-provider dispute resolution process — a formal dispute you must start within 120 calendar days of the date on the initial bill — write that deadline down the week a too-high bill arrives. Below that threshold, the difference is still a Step 3 letter item: an estimate is called good-faith for a reason, and a provider should have to explain a bill that outran it.
  • Also watch for: bills from billers never on the cast list and never disclosed — who are you, who ordered this, and where was it disclosed?
  • And lines you cannot identify — facility fees and the like. This kit won’t tell you any category is always improper, because that isn’t true. A line you cannot identify is a line you ask about, in writing, before paying.

Step 3 — The deviation letter: put it in writing, put a hold on the account

  • Phone calls open this fight; paper moves it. A dated letter, sent with proof of delivery, listing specific violations and demanding a specific status on the account, creates the file every later rung of the ladder (Step 5) will read.

DO THIS — the call that precedes it: one call first — not to argue line by line, but to establish the account’s status while the letter travels:

SAY THIS: “I’m calling about account [number], for service on [date]. I’ve received the itemized bill and I’m disputing specific charges in writing — the letter is going out [today / this week] with delivery confirmation. I’m asking you to place the account in dispute status with a billing hold now, so nothing is sent to collections while the dispute is open, and to note that I called today. Please give me your name, and confirm the hold and the note.”

Log the name, the date, and the confirmation — then send the letter even if the call went beautifully. Verbal holds evaporate; the letter is the hold’s evidence.

Print the deviation letter

DO THIS — delivery with proof: send the letter a way that proves delivery — certified mail with return receipt, the provider’s portal with a saved confirmation, or fax with the confirmation page — and log the method and date. A copy to the provider’s compliance office or patient advocate costs a stamp and is often worth it.

DO THIS — the follow-up cadence: confirm the account’s dispute status with a billing hold in writing. Then: no written acknowledgment within about two weeks? Call, reference the letter by its date and delivery proof, ask for the name of the person handling the dispute, and log it. The response date passes with no substantive answer? That silence is itself a file item — the first paragraph of whatever Step 5’s next rung receives.

KEEP THIS PAPER: Your copy of the letter, the delivery proof, the acknowledgment, every response, and the log of every follow-up. This packet, complete, is the single most valuable object this kit produces.

One calibration: a dispute letter does not erase a bill, and a billing hold is a status you request and confirm — not a spell. What the letter guarantees is that the dispute exists in writing, with dates, on both sides of the counter.

Step 4 — Negotiate what honestly remains

  • What remains after the audit is the corrected balance — genuinely yours. Pay the undisputed part. Nobody negotiates before the audit: negotiating an unaudited bill means bargaining over their errors as if they were your debts.

MOVE 1 — ASK FOR THE PROMPT-PAY / CASH DISCOUNT on the corrected balance: a balance paid in full, now, is worth a discount to almost every provider — and the discount is asked for, not offered.

SAY THIS: “The corrected balance is [amount], after the disputed charges were removed. If I pay it in full this week, what discount can you offer for prompt payment? — and if we agree on a figure, I’ll need it in writing, showing the account settled in full at that amount, before I pay.”

MOVE 2 — APPLY FOR FINANCIAL ASSISTANCE, EVEN MID-FIGHT: nonprofit hospitals must have a financial assistance policy, publish it, and accept applications — at essentially any point in the billing cycle, including while a dispute is open. A complete application generally requires the hospital to hold off on extraordinary collection actions while it’s decided.

SAY THIS: “The corrected balance is still more than I can pay. I want to apply under the hospital’s financial assistance policy. Please send me the policy and the application, tell me exactly what documentation you need, and note on the account that an application is being filed. I’ll return it complete by [date], and I want confirmation when it’s received.”

MOVE 3 — IF YOU NEED A PAYMENT PLAN, GET IT IN WRITING BEFORE THE FIRST PAYMENT: the total, the installment amount and dates, whether interest or fees apply, what happens if a payment is late, and confirmation the account stays out of collections while the plan is current.

KEEP THIS PAPER: The assistance policy and application with proof of filing and the decision; the discount agreement; the payment-plan terms and every payment confirmation. A “settled” account with no paper is an account that can be re-billed.

Lane note — welfare: this step mostly does not apply to you, and that is the shield working: a covered service’s remainder is the program’s, not yours. If what remains is a bill your lane says should never have existed, skip this step and go to Step 5: you don’t negotiate a deviation, you get it corrected.

Step 5 — The escalation ladder, assembled

The kit’s fixed order — the system’s own processes first, the regulators matched to the violation next, the attorney last.

RUNG 1 — THE PROVIDER’S BILLING OFFICE: the deviation letter (Step 3) and the negotiation (Step 4) live here. Most billing fights end on this rung.

RUNG 2 — THE PROVIDER’S COMPLIANCE OFFICE / PATIENT ADVOCATE: the office whose job is the institution’s conduct, not its collections.

SAY THIS: “This dispute has been open since [date of letter] without a substantive answer. Please transfer the file to your compliance office — or connect me with the patient advocate — and give me that office’s direct contact and a reference number for the escalation.”

RUNG 3 — THE PLAN-SIDE LADDER: when the fight is about the plan’s decision — a denied claim, a refused authorization, a miscalculated cost-share — run plan appeal (internal appeal), then external review. The billing dispute and the claim appeal are separate tracks. (Welfare lane: plan appeal first, then the state fair hearing.)

RUNG 4 — THE REGULATORS, MATCHED TO THE VIOLATION: file at the door that matches the deviation — with the packet: the letter, the log, the documents, in order.

  • Plan conduct — bad-faith denials, blown appeal deadlines, ghost-network listings → your state department of insurance. Forks: self-funded employer plans route to the U.S. Department of Labor; Medicare Advantage complaints run through Medicare itself.
  • Surprise-bill violations → the federal No Surprises help desk, run through the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS).
  • Provider billing practices generally — patterns of improper billing, charges for care never rendered → your state attorney general’s consumer protection office.
  • Hospital conduct — screening or discharge-rule violations, price-transparency failures → CMS and your state hospital-licensing agency.
  • Welfare-lane violations — improper balance billing, notice and hearing failures → your state Medicaid agency and its managed-care ombudsman first, with HHS as the federal backstop.

RUNG 5 — THE ATTORNEY, LAST: you don’t need an attorney until you’ve run through the regulations that exist for you to use. If the case reaches this rung, you arrive with a clean record and every deviation documented. (Welfare lane: your attorney rung may be free — legal aid takes these cases at no charge in every state.)

One calibration: no rung guarantees an outcome. What the ladder guarantees is sequence and weight — every rung adds a file, a deadline, and a more serious reader.

Step 6 — Collections defense

If this happens

A bill reaches a collector anyway

DO NOT PANIC-PAY: a collector’s first letter is built to produce one thing: a fast payment of an amount nobody has verified. A collector’s claim is a claim, not a verdict — and federal law gives you a specific tool for it.

The validation request. Under federal debt-collection law (the Fair Debt Collection Practices Act), a third-party collector must send you information about the debt — and if you dispute the debt in writing within the window stated in their notice — commonly 30 days from their first written notice — they must stop collecting until they verify the debt and mail you that verification: the provider, the dates, the amount, and their right to collect it.

DO THIS: the week a collector’s first letter arrives: answer it in writing, inside the window their notice states, disputing the debt and requesting validation. If the underlying bill is already disputed with the provider, say so, with the letter’s date. Send with delivery proof, keep copies, log it. Then continue the underlying fight on the ladder (Step 5) — a collector’s pause is breathing room, not a resolution.

SAY THIS (the validation request — adapt the brackets): “[Your name and address]. [Date]. Re: your letter dated [date], account/reference [number], alleged balance [amount], original provider [name]. I dispute this debt [in full / in the amount of (disputed portion)] and request validation, including an itemized statement of the charges, the dates of service, and documentation of your authority to collect this account. The underlying charges were disputed in writing with the provider on [date of your deviation letter] and that dispute [is open / resulted in (outcome)]. Please do not contact me about this debt again until you have mailed the requested validation. All further communication with me about this debt should be in writing. — [Your name.]”

If the “collector” is the provider itself, the federal statute’s main rules generally govern third-party collectors rather than a provider collecting its own bills — but state consumer-protection law, the provider’s dispute obligations, and every rung of Step 5 still apply, and the written-dispute discipline is identical.

Medical debt and your credit report — stated honestly, because it changes. As of this kit’s writing, the three credit bureaus’ voluntary practices remove paid medical collections from credit reports and exclude medical collections under $500, and medical collections generally do not appear until they are at least a year old. Those are industry policies, not permanent law — they can change, and a federal rule that would have gone further was struck down in court in 2025. Several states separately restrict or prohibit medical-debt credit reporting. Verify the current state of play — your state attorney general’s consumer page and the bureaus’ own announcements — before deciding how much a reporting threat weighs.

And the standing rule for this whole step: keep disputing from the paper, not from memory. The collector has a balance and a script. You have the itemization with the circles, the letter with the delivery receipt, the log with the names.

The mistakes — yours to not make

  1. Paying the first bill — any first bill — before the itemization, the EOB or estimate, and the match.
  2. Trying to audit a summary bill: one line, one total, nothing to check.
  3. Paying an EOB. It says it isn’t a bill; believe it.
  4. Disputing by phone only — no letter, no delivery proof, no hold confirmed.
  5. Negotiating a discount on an unaudited bill.
  6. Skipping the financial assistance application because the bill is “already in dispute” — the application runs on its own track.
  7. Making the first payment-plan installment before the plan’s terms are in your hand in writing.
  8. Ignoring a collector’s letter until its dispute window closes — the validation right has a clock, and the clock is in their notice.
  9. Panic-paying a collector on an amount nobody verified.
  10. Arguing any of it from memory instead of reading it off the log. (If your log is thin, start it today, from the paper you do have.)

The deviations — theirs to answer for

  1. A bill sent before the claim finished processing — the provider’s total presented as your balance while the plan’s share was still in transit.
  2. Duplicate charges, charges for care never received, quantities and dates the record doesn’t support.
  3. A balance bill from an out-of-network ancillary provider at an in-network facility, or above in-network cost-sharing for emergency care.
  4. A bill to a Medicaid patient for a covered service beyond allowed cost-sharing — and its QMB form.
  5. A cash patient’s final bill $400 or more above the good-faith estimate, with the dispute right nowhere mentioned on the statement.
  6. A disputed account sent to collections while a written dispute sat unanswered.
  7. A financial assistance policy that exists on paper and is never mentioned across months of collection calls.
  8. A collector pursuing a balance it cannot validate.

What “done” looks like for Stage 6:

  • Every bill in your care file has been itemized, audited, and sorted into the three piles.
  • Every deviation has been put to the provider in writing, with delivery proof, inside a dispute with a confirmed hold.
  • What honestly remains is paid, discounted in writing, covered by an assistance decision, or on written payment-plan terms.
  • Every regulator filing, every collector answer, and every outcome is in the packet, in order.

Bill Audit Worksheet

One per bill. Work it in pencil, top to bottom, with the itemized bill and your episode file open beside it. When it’s filled in, it is the audit — Step 3’s letter is written straight off it.

Your facts stay yours. Nothing here is uploaded or sent anywhere — it exists in exactly one place: your hands.

Bill / account number — the account or statement number, copied exactly as printed.

Biller — who sent this bill: facility, group, or supplier.

Dates of service — the range the bill covers.

Total billed — the bill’s total, as printed.

Your share per EOB / agreed price — what your own paper says you owe, before any disputed item is considered.
