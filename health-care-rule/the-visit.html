The visit, run like a customer — starting at the desk.

  • Most of the time the visit just happens: check in, be seen, leave with the summary.
  • This kit is a watch, not a job — it’s here for the moments that aren’t smooth.

The desk play — check-in is the first transaction

Read this when you arrive. Step 1 below is the night-before setup that makes it work.

  • You don’t arrive empty-handed: Stage 1 taught you your plan; Stage 3 verified your doctor.
  • Check-in isn’t where you find out what you have — it’s where you check whether the office has it right.

The handover

DO THIS: Hand over your insurance card and photo ID. Then ask them to confirm the visit under your exact plan name — not just the insurance company’s name.

SAY THIS: “Please check me in under [exact plan name]. Before the visit, please confirm that this office is in-network for that plan, that today’s visit is covered, and what I owe today.”

KEEP THIS PAPER: In your visit log: the desk person’s name, and their answers — especially the amount they say you owe today. Compare every answer with your plan card and your Stage 3 notes. Then follow the branch that happens.

Outcome A — It matches (what smooth looks like)

  • The office confirms your exact plan, the doctor’s network status, and the amount your plan says you owe today.

DO THIS: Proceed. Pay the confirmed amount if it’s due at check-in, get a receipt, write it in your log. Most of the time, this is the whole desk play — the rest stays in the drawer.

If this happens

Outcome B — The numbers do not match

  • The desk quotes a number that doesn’t match your plan card. Don’t accept it silently — and don’t assume you remembered yours wrong.
  • Most mismatches are screen errors: an old plan on file, the wrong plan product or tier, last year’s deductible, the wrong office location.
SAY THIS: “That doesn’t match my plan. Please recheck it under my exact plan name, [plan name], using the card I just handed you. Can you tell me what changed when you rechecked it?”

DO THIS: Fixed? Log the corrected answer and the desk person’s name, then proceed. Not fixed? Make the lobby call (Outcome C) before you pay a different amount or agree to be treated as out-of-network.

If this happens

Outcome C — “We don’t take that insurance”

  • Stop. Do not decide at the desk. A routine appointment can wait ten minutes — or be rescheduled. The pressured choice (hand over a credit card, sort it out later) is the one this play exists to prevent.
  • First, make them say which kind of “we don’t take it” this is:
SAY THIS: “When you say you don’t take it, which is it: this office never took my plan, this doctor is not in my plan’s network, or this office stopped taking it?”

IF THE PLAN’S DIRECTORY SENT YOU HERE: keep the proof first — photograph or screenshot the listing showing the doctor, this office, your plan, and today’s date. Save it with your Stage 3 notes.

KEEP THIS PAPER: The listing and your dated verification log — together they show you relied on the plan’s own information.

The lobby call

Step away from the desk and call member services — the number on the back of your card. You’re calling the referee before any money moves.

SAY THIS: “I’m standing in Dr. [name]’s office. Your plan information says they take my plan. They’re telling me they don’t. What’s going on? Please tell me whether this office is in-network for my exact plan, what I should do now, and the reference number for this call.”

KEEP THIS PAPER: Date and time, the name of who answered, the reference number, and exactly what the plan told you to do.

The call usually resolves one of three ways:

  1. The office is wrong. The practice is in-network but bills under a group name you wouldn’t recognize. The plan gives you the name on file — and the visit proceeds as in-network.
  2. The office left, and the plan’s listing was stale. The plan owns its bad information. Ask it to find you an in-network doctor and note your reliance in your file. Keep the listing and this call in your log.
  3. You have a network tier you didn’t know about. Painful — but you learned it free, in the lobby, instead of on a bill. Ask the plan to name doctors in your tier before you leave.
If this happens

“Your insurance doesn’t cover what we’re doing today”

Don’t accept that sentence as final. “Doesn’t cover” hides three different problems:

SAY THIS: “Is it not a covered benefit at all, or is it covered but needs approval first, or covered but needs a referral? Please write down the exact reason.”
  • Not a benefit at all: don’t drift into paying by default. Ask the cash price, in writing, and decide — or reschedule, or leave. The cash fork below applies.
  • Covered, but needs approval: pause or reschedule and get the prior authorization approved first. An approval chased after the service is a fight; before it, a form.
  • Covered, but needs a referral: call your primary doctor’s office — or the plan — and get the referral in place first. Reschedule if it can’t be done today.
If this happens

“We can’t verify your coverage”

  • Usually a computer said no — not that your coverage ended. A digit wrong, a name spelled differently, a plan change not yet posted, their system down.

DO THIS: Have the desk recheck the exact spelling, member number, and birth date from your card. Still failing? Make the lobby call.

SAY THIS (to the plan): “The office cannot verify my coverage. Please confirm whether my [exact plan name] coverage is active today, whether this office is in-network for it, and give me a reference number the office can use.”

KEEP THIS PAPER: The reference number and the representative’s name. If the plan confirms you’re active, ask it to document it. If the plan says you’re not active, pause routine care and fix the coverage first — don’t pay as though uninsured until you know that’s true.

If this happens

“We need money from you today” — the deposit demand

Before you pay more than your plan’s copay, separate the two numbers:

SAY THIS: “How much of that is today’s copay, and how much is an estimated deposit toward my deductible?”

DO THIS: Verify today’s true obligation with the plan — the lobby call again. Usually the copay is what’s owed today; the deductible balance is decided after the claim is processed, not at the desk. If the office insists on a deposit as policy, that’s a choice point: verify first, then decide — pay it, reschedule, or find another doctor.

KEEP THIS PAPER: If you pay anything, a receipt showing the amount, what it was for, and that it credits today’s visit. Photograph it into your care file.

If you choose this

The cash fork — only if you choose this doctor anyway

  • After the lobby call, you may still want this doctor, today. That’s your right — but for this visit you are now a cash patient, and the cash lane’s rules switch on.

DO THIS: Agree the cash price before the service, in writing: the total, what it includes, what it excludes (labs, imaging, injections, anything sent out).

SAY THIS: “I’ll pay cash for today’s visit. Before I’m seen, I need the total price in writing — what it includes and what it doesn’t — and afterward I’ll need an itemized receipt I can submit myself.”

KEEP THIS PAPER: The written price, and afterward an itemized receipt or superbill (provider, date, services and codes, amount paid). PPO: keep it to submit the visit yourself. HMO warning: the visit likely won’t count toward your deductible and the plan may pay nothing — verify with the plan before you choose, if the amount matters.

The walk-away door

It stays open the whole time. You can say:

SAY THIS: “Thank you. I’m not going to decide this at the desk. I’ll find a doctor in my network.”

Then leave, and use Stage 3 to choose again. For a routine visit there’s no penalty for walking away — the appointment is not a contract. You are the customer.

Katie’s rule for this stage

THE RULE: the desk must bill you under the plan you actually hold. The plan’s directory has to be accurate, and an office bills under the contracts it holds for your exact plan — not just your insurer’s name in general.

THE CHECK: compare every desk answer to your plan card and your Stage 3 notes. Log who said what: the desk person’s name, the amount quoted, the plan representative’s name and reference number.

THE MEANING: that dated record is what the bill is later measured against. Check it like a receipt: calmly, as a routine — verification, never accusation.

The steps

Step 1 — Before the visit: arrive with the visit already set up

FIRST — CONFIRM THE VISIT IS SET UP RIGHT:

DO THIS: The day before (or when you book), confirm with the office: the appointment is in their system under your name and coverage; any referral or authorization your lane requires is on file, not “being handled”; and what you’ll owe at check-in.

SAY THIS: “I’m calling to confirm tomorrow’s appointment. Is the referral [or authorization] on file for this visit? And what will I owe at check-in?”

Log the answer, with the name of who gave it.

Lane note — insured: in an HMO or POS plan, a specialist visit usually needs a referral — sometimes a prior authorization on top — and a visit with neither can be denied outright. In a PPO, you can usually self-refer, but the service can still need prior authorization. Confirm approval and get the authorization number before the visit.
Lane note — welfare: specialist visits generally run through your primary doctor’s referral; some services need the plan’s prior authorization — your member handbook says which. If the office says the referral hasn’t arrived, call your primary doctor or plan today.
Lane note — cash: no referral system, no authorization — one discipline instead: re-confirm the agreed visit price before the visit. “I’m confirming our agreed cash price for tomorrow’s visit is still [amount], for [what it includes].” If the answer moved, you learn it while you can still reschedule.

SECOND — WRITE YOUR QUESTIONS DOWN BEFORE YOU GO: the visit is short and the room sets its own pace. Paper doesn’t compete — it waits. Write your questions the night before, in the order they matter, and bring two copies if you can: one to hold, one to hand over.

THIRD — BRING THE KIT, AND KNOW TODAY’S NUMBER:

DO THIS: Bring: your card (cash lane: your written agreed-price confirmation) · your medication list from the bottles · your written question list · your visit log · a pen. And know what today is expected to cost you — so a different number at check-in is a question you ask on the spot.

Step 2 — The signature discipline: read the packet like a customer

  • The intake packet is several different documents wearing one cover sheet. A customer reads it. A captive signs it.

THE RULES THAT GOVERN EVERY PAGE:

  1. Never sign a blank form, and never sign a form you haven’t read. The actual words, on the actual page. If reading takes ten minutes, it takes ten minutes.
  2. You may read first, at your own pace, and ask what a form is before you sign it. “What is this one for?” is a complete question.
  3. You are entitled to copies of everything you sign. Ask before you leave, or photograph each signed page on the spot. The copies go in your care file the same day.

THE PAGES THEMSELVES — WHAT EACH ONE ACTUALLY IS:

  • Consent to treat. Permission to examine and treat you — the one the visit actually needs. Usually general; each procedure carries its own consent conversation (Step 3).
  • Financial responsibility / assignment of benefits. The money page: the practice may bill your plan — and, read this twice, you commonly agree that whatever the plan doesn’t pay is yours. It cannot lawfully override your coverage’s protections, but it’s written broadly and quoted back at patients later. Signing it is normal. Signing it unread is the mistake.
  • Notice of Privacy Practices acknowledgment. A receipt — it acknowledges you were offered the privacy notice. It is not a waiver of your privacy rights and not consent to anything else.
Recognize this page

The arbitration agreement — the page that hides

  • Some packets include an agreement that disputes — in some versions, even malpractice claims — go to a private arbitrator instead of a court, waiving a jury trial. It often sits in the packet looking like every other page.
  • In many practices and states it’s optional — declinable, or revocable within a window, without losing your care. In some places the rules differ. This kit doesn’t tell you to sign or refuse.
  • The move: find it, recognize it, ask the question below out loud, and note the answer and the answerer’s name in your log.
SAY THIS (the arbitration question — and for any form you don’t understand): “Before I sign this one — is this required for me to be treated today? What happens if I don’t sign it?” Then, if you decline anything: “Please note in my chart that I declined this form, and give me a copy of the packet showing what I did and didn’t sign.”
SAY THIS (copies): “I’ll need copies of everything I signed today — or I can photograph the pages now, whichever is easier for you.”

KEEP THIS PAPER: The full packet as you signed it — including any page you declined, noted as declined — filed with the date. Oral explanations go in your visit log with the explainer’s name.

One honest boundary: in a true emergency, forms get signed after stabilization, and the reading discipline applies when you’re able — Stage 5 covers the hospital version. Nothing here is a reason to delay urgent care.

Step 3 — During the visit: who is in the room, and the two questions

AGENDA FIRST: open with your written list. Hand over the second copy if you brought one; say what matters most today before the room sets its own agenda.

KNOW WHO IS IN THE ROOM: you’re entitled to know who each person is and why they’re there. For people not essential to your care — students observing, observers — you may generally decline and be examined without them. Not knowing is the only wrong answer.

SAY THIS: “Before we start — could everyone introduce themselves? Who’s treating me today, and is anyone here to observe?” And if you want someone out: “I’d prefer to keep this to the care team today. Thank you.”

LANGUAGE ACCESS IS AN OBLIGATION, NOT A FAVOR: if you need an interpreter — spoken language, or sign-language / communication access — federal law obliges nearly all providers to provide it free. Ask in advance, when you book. If you arrive and it isn’t there, note it in your log and ask to reschedule with access arranged.

SAY THIS (at booking): “I’ll need a [language / sign-language] interpreter for this visit. Please arrange it and confirm it’s arranged before my appointment.”

Log the confirmation.

THE CUSTOMER’S TWO QUESTIONS — for every test, scan, procedure, referral, or new service proposed in the room, asked before yes:

  1. “What is this for?” — so the purpose lands in your log in plain words. A service nobody explained out loud can’t be tracked, verified, or recognized on a bill.
  2. “What will it cost me — and is it covered and authorized?” — while the answer can still change the plan: a different facility, a different day, an authorization filed first, a cash price quoted.
Lane note — cash: the second question is the whole lane in one sentence: get the price before yes. If the office can’t price it on the spot, the schedulable version can almost always wait a day for the good-faith estimate.
Lane note — insured: “covered” and “authorized” are different words — a covered benefit can still need prior authorization. If the answer is “we’ll submit it,” confirm approval and get the number before the service happens.
Lane note — welfare: the second question’s welfare form is “Is this covered by my plan, and is it authorized?” A service your program covers is not your bill. A service proposed outside the program — a private-pay extra — should be named as such, in writing, before you agree to owe for it.
If this happens

The preventive-visit trap

  • A visit billed as preventive is priced as preventive only while it stays preventive. If a new problem gets worked on in the room, that part can be billed as a separate regular visit — with its own cost to you.
  • Neither answer is wrong. Being surprised by it is the trap — so ask out loud, before you leave:
SAY THIS: “This visit was booked as preventive. If we work on this problem today, will part of today be billed as a regular visit — and what would that cost me?”

KEEP THIS PAPER: The answer, in your visit log, with the name of who gave it. Stage 6 measures the bill against it.

A REFERRAL OR ORDER OPENS A SECOND PLAY: the moment the doctor says “I’m referring you” or “I’m ordering this,” Stage 5’s referral play takes over — get the order on paper before you leave, verify the destination yourself, settle who files the approval. Run it there.

LEAVE WITH THE SUMMARY: every visit generates an after-visit summary (AVS). Always leave with it — printed, or confirmed posted to your portal — and photograph it into your care file. If it says something you don’t recognize, raise it now, while the visit is still today.

KEEP THIS PAPER: The after-visit summary, every time, no exceptions.

Step 4 — Checkout and after the visit: close the loop the same day

AT CHECKOUT: confirm what you paid at check-in was credited to today’s visit; get a receipt for anything paid at checkout; confirm how the promised follow-ups and referrals will be handled — who sends them, and by when.

WRITE YOUR VISIT LOG ENTRY THE SAME DAY: ten minutes, the worksheet at the end of this stage: who you saw, what was decided, and — the field that matters most — what was promised, each promise with its date. Memory edits promises kindly within a week. The log doesn’t.

VERIFY THE PROMISES ACTUALLY HAPPENED: the system has no alarm for a referral never sent or a result never communicated.

  • Referrals: within a few days, confirm the referral went out and the specialist’s office received it. A referral said in the room and never sent is a deviation — fixed fastest by the call below.
  • Results: the office that ordered a test owns communicating its result — “no news is good news” is a custom, not a rule. When the promised window passes with no result, call.
SAY THIS (the follow-up call): “I was seen on [date]. At the visit I was told [the referral to Dr. ___ would be sent / my results would be ready by ___]. I haven’t [received confirmation / heard anything]. Can you check today and tell me the status? — and your name, please, for my log.”

USE THE PORTAL FOR THE RECORD: portal messages are dated, written, and kept — the three things phone calls are not. Where a call is necessary, your log entry is the call’s paper.

PRESCRIPTIONS ARE PAPERWORK TOO: the choice of pharmacy is the patient’s — an office default is a default, not an assignment. Check before the first fill:

Lane note — insured: your plan’s formulary decides what the drug costs you — tier, prior authorization, preferred pharmacies. One call or portal lookup before the fill; lower-tier alternatives are a conversation your doctor’s office has every day.
Lane note — welfare: confirm the pharmacy is in your plan and the drug is covered. A denial is a written-notice, appeal-rights event — not a counter conversation to lose.
Lane note — cash: the same drug can differ in price by multiples between pharmacies on the same street. Ask the cash price before the fill, at more than one pharmacy for anything ongoing, and ask about discount cards and programs.

CORRECTING THE RECORD — THE AMENDMENT RIGHT: under HIPAA you may request an amendment of a wrong chart entry — in writing, stating what’s wrong and what it should say. Providers generally have 60 days to act, with one limited extension they must explain in writing (verify the current rule and any faster state deadline). If they refuse, the refusal must be explained in writing — and your written statement of disagreement must be included in your record and accompany it when the disputed information is shared. A wrong entry you can’t get removed, you can still get answered — permanently, in the file itself.

SAY THIS (amendment request, in writing — adapt the brackets): “[Your name, date of birth]. I am requesting an amendment to my medical record. The entry dated [date] states [quote or summarize the error]. This is incorrect because [the fact]. I am requesting it be corrected to [the correction]. Please confirm receipt of this request and respond within the required timeframe. — [Signature, date.]”

KEEP THIS PAPER: Your visit log, the AVS, every promise-verification note, amendment requests and their answers — filed by date. Stage 5 runs on the referrals tracked here; Stage 6 runs on all of it.

What comes next — Stage 5’s referral play

When the doctor says “I’m referring you,” a new episode begins — and the office usually prepares the referral after you leave. Stage 5 opens with the play for that gap: the three commitments to get before you leave, verifying the destination yourself, and the rule that the appointment doesn’t happen until the referral has arrived, any authorization is on file, and the destination is verified in-network.

The mistakes — yours to not make

  1. Arriving with your questions in your head — and leaving with half of them unasked.
  2. Assuming the referral or authorization was on file because someone said it would be.
  3. Signing the intake packet unread — including the one page that wasn’t like the others.
  4. Signing anything blank, or any form whose purpose nobody could explain.
  5. Walking out without copies of what you signed, and without the after-visit summary.
  6. Saying yes to a test or service before asking what it’s for and what it will cost you.
  7. Conducting a visit through a family member’s interpreting when the free, obligated service was available.
  8. Trusting “we’ll send that referral” as a completed event.
  9. Letting a wrong chart entry stand. The amendment process is a form, not a fight.
  10. Writing no visit log — so every promise exists only as a memory at bill time.

The deviations — theirs to answer for

  1. A visit conducted without the referral or authorization the office itself said was required — then billed to the patient as the patient’s failure.
  2. An arbitration agreement buried in a routine packet, its optional nature never mentioned.
  3. Refusal — or visible penalty — when a patient reads forms, declines an optional form, or asks for copies.
  4. Interpreter services not arranged after a clear advance request.
  5. An after-visit summary listing services not performed, or omitting promised follow-ups and referrals.
  6. A referral stated in the visit and never transmitted; results never communicated within the promised window.
  7. A chart entry the provider refuses to amend and refuses to answer with your statement of disagreement in the record.
  8. A prescription routed or priced in a way nobody disclosed, when asking would have decided it differently.

What “done” looks like for Stage 4:

  • You arrived set up, questions on paper; you signed only what you read and kept copies of all of it.
  • You know whether an arbitration page was in your packet, and what you decided about it.
  • You know who was in your room, and what every proposed service was for and would cost.
  • You left with the summary in hand and a log entry written the same day — every promise dated, follow-ups calendared.

Visit Log — the worksheet

One page per visit. Fill the top half in the room or the parking lot; finish it the same evening. File it behind the visit’s after-visit summary.

Your facts stay yours. Nothing here is uploaded or sent anywhere — it exists in exactly one place: your hands.

Date — the date of the visit. Every clock and every promise counts from here.

Provider — who you saw: name, practice, and anyone else in the room whose role mattered.

Reason for the visit — why you went, in your words.

What was decided — tests ordered, prescriptions started or changed, follow-ups set. Compare against the after-visit summary before you file it.

What was promised — each promise, with its date: the referral, the results, the follow-up. The most important field on the page — a promise with a date can be verified.

Paper received — after-visit summary, copies of signed forms, estimates, instructions. Missing paper is a call the next morning.

My cost — what you paid at the visit, and what you were told to expect after.
