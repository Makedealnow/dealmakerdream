The complete DIY system for disputing credit report errors — the services, the letters, the deadlines, and what to send back when they reply — assembled from a real dispute campaign, run start to finish.

The problem

Your credit report has errors. You already know that part.

What you don't have is three free weekends to learn how any of this actually works: which bureaus really hold your file (it's not just the big three), how to freeze the ones nobody's heard of, how to get certified letters mailed without a post office line, which date the legal clock actually starts on, and what to do when a bureau writes back claiming you "didn't provide enough information" — about the letter where you provided everything.

Most people quit right there. Not because the dispute is hard. Because the logistics are a research project.

What this kit is

One download that hands you the finished research:

  • Start-Here roadmap — the whole campaign as one numbered sequence, in the order that works: pull every report → freeze everything → dispute the bureaus and the company reporting the data → track → answer the stalls → escalate only if they miss.
  • Build Your Case Pack — a five-question self-assessment you answer from your own reports. It routes you by situation — wrong account status, post-bankruptcy reporting errors, inquiries you never authorized one by one, a personal-information mess, a furnisher who won't fix it — to exactly the letters, playbook sections, and tracker rows your case needs, and tells you what you can skip. It includes two letters most kits don't have: the inquiry-authorization dispute and the specialty-bureau personal-information cleanup.
  • The Services Stack — a virtual mailbox explained (what it does, what it typically costs), and real mail-it-for-you services that print, stuff, and send your letters — Certified Mail included — from an uploaded PDF, with current approximate prices. No printer, no post office, no envelopes.
  • The tracking rule — why USPS tracking can show a letter "still moving" after it already arrived, and the three kinds of delivery proof that actually hold up.
  • Three fill-in letters, generalized from real ones that were mailed and answered: the bureau dispute letter, the direct-to-the-furnisher letter (the step almost nobody takes — and the one that decides everything), and the "Not a New Dispute" response for the brush-off stall.
  • The Deadline Tracker — a fill-in table plus the clock rules in plain English: a bureau's 30 days runs from receipt, not mailing; adding information mid-investigation can hand them 15 more days; a mortgage servicer's clocks are different, and run separately.
  • The Playbook — what every kind of reply actually means (the acknowledgment, the "insufficient information" stall, the form letter asking for what you already sent, the "reported accurately" position letter, the rubber-stamp "verified") and exactly what to send back for each.
  • Plain-English glossary — furnisher, RESPA, QWR, discharge code, freeze vs. fraud alert, and the rest, defined like a person would explain them.
