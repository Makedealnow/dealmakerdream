The Warranty Test

They sell it because the math works for them. This kit hands you the math.

How to tell — in five minutes, with your own numbers — whether an extended warranty is worth buying, or a bad deal wearing a reassuring name.

A plain-English system for analyzing any extended warranty, service contract, or protection plan before you pay for it: what these contracts really are, the five-minute test that decides most of them, the fine print that decides the rest, the scripts for the pressure moments, and the self-insurance move that keeps the money in your house instead.

The problem

The offer never arrives at a calm moment. It arrives at the register, in the last ten seconds of a purchase. In the finance office, at the end of a car deal, quoted as a monthly crumb inside a payment you've already accepted. In a mailer dressed up like a bill — "final notice" — about a warranty you may not even have. Or at renewal time, when a plan you barely remember buying asks to continue by doing nothing at all.

And it's always priced to sound small: a monthly figure, a fraction of the purchase price, "peace of mind." What nobody in that room will ever do is reassemble the real numbers — the total cost, the interest if it's financed, the claim cap, the deductible's shape, the years that overlap coverage you already own for free — and let you compare them to the one repair the contract would actually pay for.

That's not an accident. An extended warranty is insurance, and insurance is priced so that, across all the contracts sold, the seller pays out less in claims than they take in. The average buyer gets back less than they paid. The only question that matters is whether your contract, on your numbers, is one of the rare exceptions — and nobody at the counter is going to run that test for you.

What this kit is

One download that teaches you to run the test yourself, on any offer, in about five minutes:

  • The Honest Machine — what an extended warranty actually is: where your premium dollar goes (claims, the seller's commission, the administrator's margin), why the pitch arrives exactly when it does, and the double-coverage trick — plans that start today while your free factory warranty is still running, so a "five-year plan" may only add two real years.
  • The Test — five questions, one verdict rule, and three fully worked examples: a washer plan that fails, a service contract and GAP policy bundled into a car loan (what "about $60 a month" really costs across 120 months), and the known-lemon furnace that honestly passes. Plus the three wrong numbers almost every bad decision is made with: the monthly payment, the replacement cost, and the price of the item.
  • The 12-point fine-print checklist — named parts vs. exclusionary coverage, wear-and-tear and pre-existing-condition exclusions, maintenance-record traps, deductible shapes, claim caps, the prior-authorization trap — and how to read a whole contract in ten minutes, in the order that matters. Includes the home-warranty claim game: denial by attribution, the cheap patch, the lowball cash-out.
  • The Repair Fund — the self-insurance move: bank every premium you decline, and the fund — not a claims department — decides your repairs. It covers every failure, including the ones contracts exclude. With the honest caveats about starting from empty, and the standing household rule that ends the register negotiation for good.
