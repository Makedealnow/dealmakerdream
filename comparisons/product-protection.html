The Warranty Test, Applied

Product protection plans, run through the test

Seven real appliance and electronics protection plans — the kind pitched at the register, on your phone bill, and at the warehouse club — priced from the sellers' own published numbers and run through the same five-question Warranty Test as our kit. Full math, full caps, and a verdict on every row.

The plans, tested

Plan A — The warehouse-club plan Verdict: Pass

Sold alongside the appliance at a warehouse club.

What it coversMechanical and electrical failure in normal use — major appliances, TVs, and computers.
Term3 years, one payment up front.
Total true cost$119.99 for an appliance priced $1,000–$2,000; $89.99 under $1,000; up to $399.99 for large kitchen suites. A TV under $500: $34.99. Computer accident protection under $500: $69.99. (Published warehouse-channel prices, November 2026.)
Deductible$0 on most plans. (This seller's phone plans carry a deductible of about $149 — a weaker row of its catalog, and not the plan scored here.)
Payout cap$2,000 per claim; $5,000 per 12 months (electronics terms).
Exclusions that matterWear-and-tear on standard plans; loss and theft; intentional damage.
ReputationThe category's institutional player, owned by a major national insurer. Complaints exist — claims friction is the recurring theme — and its record carries none of the regulator settlements seen elsewhere in this industry.

The math: $120 once, against a realistic $600–$1,500 refrigerator repair-or-replace event in years 2–3, after the factory warranty ends. One real failure returns roughly 5–10× the plan's cost. That shape — one payment, no deductible, a payout many times the price — is the rarest thing in this industry.

Plan B — The everything-bundle Verdict: Pass

One subscription that covers a phone plus a shelf of other gear.

What it coversAccidental damage and theft — one phone plus 25 other items under a single plan.
TermMonth-to-month subscription.
Total true cost$15 a month — $180 a year. A phone-only tier runs $6–$12 a month.
DeductibleDamage $29–$99 depending on the phone model; theft/replacement $75–$99. (Compare the carrier theft deductibles in Plan F: $249–$279.)
Payout cap$2,000 per claim; claims unlimited on this tier.
ReputationA small complaint footprint: 75 complaints in three years at the Better Business Bureau — a rounding error next to the big warranty names. Straight talk: it's a membership-style plan backed by a specialty insurer, rather than one of the household-name insurance companies.

The math: one phone replacement — an $800–$1,200 value — costs you a $99 deductible at most. That single bad day returns roughly 4–6 years of premiums, and the same plan is standing behind 25 other items the whole time.

One plan that follows everything you buy online.

What it coversAccident and failure protection on unlimited eligible online purchases, all under one plan.
TermMonth-to-month subscription.
Total true cost$9.99 a month — about $120 a year.
Deductible$0. Claims are unlimited.
Payout cap$2,500 per year, total. That cap is the whole story of this row — read it twice.

A household that buys several mid-value items a year — and breaks things — can come out ahead. A careful household is paying $120 a year for a capped benefit, and for that household this row fails. Plan D — The up-front-pricing seller Verdict: Borderline

Publishes its prices before checkout — a rare habit in this category.

What it coversAppliance failure plans priced by the item, plus a phone plan covering accidental damage, liquid, cracked screens, and mechanical/electrical failure including the battery.
TermAppliance plans run 2–3 years; the phone plan is month-to-month.
Total true cost3-year refrigerator plan about $180 (published example). Small appliances are priced by item — a 2-year plan on a $50 small appliance runs $10. Phone plan $9.99 a month ($120 a year). Across the catalog, prices run roughly 40–60% below register plans.
Deductible$0 on many appliance plans. Phone plan, per published summaries: $0 for a screen, $75 for other repairs, $150 for a replacement — check your quote on a high-end phone, where one reviewer reports a $99 deductible.
Payout capPhone payouts are capped at the device's price, with 2 claims per rolling 12 months. The phone plan covers damage, not theft or loss, and carries a 90-day waiting period unless the phone is new.
ReputationRated 4.4 out of 5 across 1,286 reviews on a major consumer review site, with fast payouts the dominant praise. One watch item: a reported mid-term price jump on an appliance plan.

The math: at register-plan prices cut roughly in half, with $0 deductibles on many plans, failure-prone appliances — laundry pairs, refrigerators — land near break-even to positive. The phone plan roughly breaks even in a screen-and-repair year, and pays $0 the day a phone is lost or stolen. The appliance side of this row leans pass; the phone side holds it at borderline.

Plan E — The phone maker's own plan Verdict: Borderline — the benchmark

Sold by the manufacturer, priced against its own repair prices. This row is the benchmark.

What it coversAccidental damage on the maker's flagship phones — and, on the higher tier, theft and loss.
TermMonth-to-month.
Total true costAbout $9.99–$13.99 a month with theft and loss included (published figures; your phone model sets the exact price).
Deductible$29 for a screen repair.

The math: genuinely fair for a new phone, because the payout is measured against the manufacturer's own repair prices — a cracked screen or a replaced phone is worth close to what the plan charges to cover it. This row anchors the table: it's what "fair" looks like, so every other row has something honest to be measured against.

The protection line already sitting on most phone bills.

What it coversDamage, theft, and loss for one phone line — sold by the major carriers, administered by a third-party company.
TermMonth-to-month, per line.

DeductibleTheft/loss deductible $249–$279.

Plan G — The register upsell Verdict: Fails

The plan offered in the last ten seconds at the checkout counter.

What it coversFailure protection on the item in your cart, quoted as a fraction of its price.
TermTypically 2–5 years, one payment at the register.
Total true cost15–35% of the item's price, priced at the counter — with the full terms rarely shown before you pay.
DeductibleVaries, and is often sitting in terms you haven't been shown.

The math: structurally the worst true cost per dollar of coverage on this page. This is the baseline row every other plan is measured against — and the exact pitch the Warranty Test was built for.

How we ran the test

Each row went through the kit's five questions, on the seller's published numbers:

  1. What's the total true cost? Every premium across the full term, plus the deductible — and the interest, if the plan is financed.
  2. What's the worst repair it would actually pay for? The realistic major repair, after the deductible and under the plan's claim cap — not the headline coverage figure.
  3. How likely is that failure inside the coverage window? A plan that starts while the free factory warranty is still running is selling years you already own.
  4. Could you pay that repair yourself without real hardship? If yes, the kit's rule says the premium is better banked in a Repair Fund.
  5. The verdict rule: a plan earns a Pass only when the payout beats the true cost by a wide margin, the failure is a realistic risk, and the loss isn't one you could simply absorb. Everything else lands at Borderline or Fails — and the fine print (covered-parts lists, exclusions, caps) settles the borderline rows.

Bottom line

Plan A wins this page. A one-time payment of about $120 against a realistic $600–$1,500 repair, with a $0 deductible, is the rarest shape in this industry: a plan whose best day pays back five to ten times its cost, and whose worst day costs you $120. For phones and a household's worth of mixed electronics, Plan B is the pick — the only phone row where a single bad day returns years of premiums.

Notice the fingerprints the two passing rows share: the price is published before you buy, the deductible is $0 or close to it, and the cap is printed where you can see it. And notice the pattern at the other end: the closer a plan is sold to the moment of purchase (Plan G), or the more quietly it rides a bill you're already paying (Plan F), the worse the math gets.
